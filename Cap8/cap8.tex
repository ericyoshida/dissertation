This dissertation addressed the perception (\emph{Sense}) layer of urban UAV
detect-and-avoid: detecting and tracking obstacles in three dimensions, in real
time, from a platform that is itself moving. Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}
isolated three properties that make this problem hard, namely an observer in
motion, depth that is uncertain and range-dependent, and a scarcity of annotated
three-dimensional aerial data, and the work confronted all three through a single
artifact: the modular camera--point-cloud pipeline of
Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}, in which a CNN detector seeds a viewing frustum, a
point-cloud network lifts each detection into a metric box, and a recursive
Bayesian filter turns the stream of detections into calibrated tracks. The three
self-contained studies of Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking}--\ref{cha:airsim} are not
three separate works but three contributions to that one pipeline. This closing
chapter revisits what each study established, draws out the threads that bind
them into one system, states plainly what remains unsolved, and sets the agenda
for an integrated onboard capability.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

The dissertation's contributions follow the \emph{one vision, three
contributions} structure: a systematic positioning of the problem, then three
studies presented here in the order of the pipeline they build, from the
detection front-end that produces the measurements to the tracking stage that
consumes them and its real-world generalization.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Positioning the problem (Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}).}
  A systematic literature review following the PRISMA, Kitchenham, and Wohlin
  protocols screened $183$ unique records and synthesized $173$ studies. Its
  centerpiece, the uniqueness matrix, established that of the nineteen closest
  adjacent works \emph{none} simultaneously targets a UAV, employs a
  frustum-to-PointNet detector with camera--LiDAR fusion, and terminates in an
  integrated tracker for urban sense-and-avoid. The review thereby substantiated,
  rather than merely asserted, the gap that the dissertation fills, and explained
  its timing through the recent maturation of light UAV-grade LiDAR, the absence of
  a real aerial 3D benchmark, and the non-trivial domain shift from ground to air.

  \item \textbf{The detection front-end and its data (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}).}
  The two stages that feed the tracker were designed, trained, and evaluated on a
  purpose-built synthetic aerial dataset. A pixel-exact synthetic-composition
  scheme circumvented the simulator's unreliable annotation interfaces and trained
  a 2D detector to $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}=0.994$, while a geometric construction
  supplied trustworthy 3D supervision. The study's principal empirical finding is
  that \emph{foreground extraction, not the box regressor, governs frustum
  localization}: removing the background that dominates the point-cloud
  normalization, whether by a depth-band heuristic or a learned segmentation, took
  the 3D error from ${\approx}3$~m to ${\approx}1$~m. The chapter also delivered the
  first empirical comparison of the two-stage frustum design against the
  single-stage voxel paradigm on aerial data, exposing a clean recall--precision
  trade-off: the frustum pipeline is precise (AMOTA $0.81$, precision $0.90$) but
  inherits its 2D detector's recall ceiling (${\approx}0.49$), whereas a
  learned-feature voxel detector recalls far more targets ($0.80$) and localizes
  them best ($0.87$~m RMSE) at the cost of noisier identities, with a late fusion
  recovering much of both. Crucially, the localization error is essentially flat
  with range, mirroring the range-bounded uncertainty of the
  Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} filter and confirming that detector and tracker are
  matched by design.

  \item \textbf{The tracking stage and the reference-frame question
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}).}
  The tracking filter was formulated for a moving platform observing a
  target through range--bearing measurements, in two natural variants: one carrying
  the object state in the global inertial frame, the other in the local sensor
  frame. The central result is an \emph{equivalence theorem}: under a known
  platform pose and rotation-invariant (isotropic) process noise, the two
  formulations are mathematically identical, a claim proven analytically and
  verified numerically to $1.7\times10^{-13}$~m, with both filters attaining the
  same RMSE of $0.228$~m. This corrects the framing of the earlier conference
  report \cite{delima2025fusion}: the apparent superiority of the global frame was
  an artifact of a local filter that omitted the inter-frame state reorientation,
  not an intrinsic advantage. The chapter further characterized precisely the
  regimes where a genuine difference arises, namely anisotropic process noise
  (where the frame that diagonalizes $\bm{Q}$ wins, a $41\%$ RMSE difference under
  strong directional noise and platform yaw) and, most consequentially, an
  uncertain platform pose. Benchmarking against the posterior Cram\'er--Rao bound
  confirmed the filter to be near-optimal, with an estimation error that grows
  gracefully with range, exactly the behavior a collision-avoidance system
  requires.

  \item \textbf{Generality of the framework on real data
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}).}
  The same constant-velocity EKF was then taken out of simulation and onto a real
  quadrotor at TU Berlin, with two of the simulation's assumptions removed at once:
  the metric range--bearing sensor became a single monocular camera (depth
  recovered through a ground-plane constraint), and the platform pose became an
  on-board estimate rather than a known quantity. Tracking a freely walking person
  during flights with full $360^\circ$ yaw rotations, the system attained a median
  horizontal error of $4.3$~m over a ${\sim}12$~m arena from monocular cues alone
  and without GNSS, against a LiDAR-referenced ground truth and with no post-hoc
  alignment. More important than the accuracy was the consistency result: this is
  exactly the uncertain-pose regime that Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} predicted would
  break the frame equivalence, and propagating the six-degree-of-freedom camera-pose
  covariance into the innovation through an extrinsic Jacobian proved
  \emph{necessary}, not cosmetic. An ablation showed that omitting this term leaves
  the filter overconfident by four orders of magnitude (mean NIS $3.4\times10^{4}$
  against the consistent value of $2$), whereas including it restores statistical
  consistency. The study thus demonstrated both the generality of the estimator
  across sensing modalities and the condition under which that generality holds: an
  honest model of where the uncertainty comes from.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{Synthesis.}
Read together, the studies make a single claim about \emph{one} system. The
common thread is calibrated uncertainty: every stage was designed to report not
only where an obstacle is but how trustworthy that estimate is, because the
downstream avoidance logic reasons about risk. This is why the pipeline
terminates in a Bayesian filter rather than a regressor, why
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} analyzed the filter's \emph{consistency} against the
Cram\'er--Rao bound and not merely its accuracy, and why the central real-world
result of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} is a consistency result. The second thread
is geometric, interpretable fusion: the image localizes the search and the point
cloud supplies the depth, keeping each modality independently trainable and each
stage independently validatable, which is precisely what let the tracker be
proven correct in isolation, the detector be evaluated on its own metrics, and
the same estimator serve both a 3D LiDAR-fused obstacle and a monocular person.
Finally, the boundary that
Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:monocular} share, known versus uncertain
platform pose, is not a coincidence of two projects but the single hinge on which
the framework turns from its equivalent to its divergent regime.

\section{Limitations}

The contributions are best understood alongside an honest account of what the
work does not yet establish.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The pipeline is validated stage-wise, not yet end-to-end on
  hardware.} Each stage was exercised against its own ground truth, by design of
  the modular architecture, but no single experiment runs the full
  camera--point-cloud chain on a real UAV against real three-dimensional
  obstacles. The obstacle tracker was validated in simulation
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}), the detection front-end in simulation
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}), and only the monocular tracker on real flights
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}).
  \item \textbf{The detection front-end is trained and tested purely in
  simulation.} The AirSim study sidesteps the data-scarcity obstacle but inherits
  a sim-to-real gap: the detectors have not been confronted with the noise,
  lighting, and appearance statistics of real aerial footage, so their reported
  accuracy is an upper bound on real-world performance.
  \item \textbf{Recall, not precision, limits the two-stage design.} The frustum
  pipeline's recall falls to $0.31$--$0.58$ under tree clutter and fog, inherited
  almost entirely from the 2D stage. In a collision-avoidance application a missed
  obstacle is the most dangerous failure, so this ceiling is a genuine
  limitation, only partially relieved by the voxel and fusion variants.
  \item \textbf{The monocular tracker rests on a ground-plane assumption.} The
  single-camera lift trades the unobservable depth for a known target height; any
  violation of that assumption, or any error in the foot-point detection that
  stands in for ground contact, maps directly to a planar error that grows with
  range. The largest errors concentrate during the platform's most aggressive
  rotations, where the estimated pose is least accurate. The study also rests on a
  single scene and a single target class.
  \item \textbf{The multi-object layer is not analyzed with the rigor of the
  single-target filter.} The data association and track lifecycle of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} are a heuristic, SORT-style layer; the
  Cram\'er--Rao and consistency analysis of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} applies to
  the per-track filter, not to the association problem, whose identity switches
  remain a measured weakness of the voxel detectors.
  \item \textbf{Pose-uncertainty propagation has not been carried into the 3D
  pipeline.} The extrinsic-aware innovation covariance of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} was derived for the monocular projective
  measurement; the 3D detection-fed tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} still
  treats the platform pose as known.
  \item \textbf{No real-time, embedded demonstration.} All inference was run
  offline; the onboard-compute budget that an embedded sense-and-avoid system
  imposes (Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}) has not yet been met.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}

These limitations chart a concrete agenda, ordered roughly from the most
immediate to the most ambitious.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Closing the sim-to-real gap.} The most direct next step is to
  validate the Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} detectors on real aerial footage, exactly
  as the monocular tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} was validated on real
  flights. Domain randomization and domain adaptation during synthetic training,
  together with the collection or annotation of a modest real aerial 3D set, are
  the natural means; the foreground-extraction finding suggests that robustifying
  the segmentation to real clutter is where the effort should concentrate.
  \item \textbf{End-to-end integration on a single platform.} The stages should be
  composed into one system running the full
  2D~$\rightarrow$~frustum~$\rightarrow$~3D~$\rightarrow$~EKF chain on a real UAV,
  closing the loop that the modular validation deliberately left open, and
  deployed on embedded UAV-grade hardware within a real-time budget. The
  recall ceiling motivates fielding the late frustum--voxel fusion rather than the
  frustum pipeline alone.
  \item \textbf{Tighter detector--tracker coupling.} The detector's own
  uncertainty should feed the filter's measurement covariance $\bm{R}$ rather than
  a fixed model, and the heuristic association should give way to an
  uncertainty-aware, possibly learned, scheme that receives the rigorous treatment
  the single-target filter already enjoys.
  \item \textbf{Pose uncertainty in the full 3D pipeline.} The pose-covariance
  propagation proven necessary in Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} should be carried
  into the spherical-measurement tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}, instantiating
  the uncertain-pose divergent regime of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} for the 3D
  case and yielding a tracker that is consistent under the real navigation
  solution rather than an assumed-known pose.
  \item \textbf{Collaborative multi-UAV perception.} Because the tracker consumes
  an abstract measurement with a calibrated covariance, tracks estimated by
  several platforms can in principle be fused into a shared situational picture,
  extending the single-observer awareness of this work to a cooperative one.
  \item \textbf{Closing the loop to \emph{Avoid}.} The calibrated tracks this
  pipeline emits were designed for a risk-aware avoidance layer; coupling the
  perception output to trajectory planning and control would complete the
  detect-and-avoid capability of which this dissertation built the \emph{Sense}
  half.
\end{itemize}

\section{Concluding Remarks}

Safe autonomous flight in the urban canyon will be certified, ultimately, on the
trustworthiness of the aircraft's situational awareness, and trustworthiness
means not only accurate estimates but estimates that know how uncertain they are.
This dissertation contributed the perception layer of that capability as a single
modular camera--point-cloud pipeline and validated its components from three
complementary angles: a rigorous, near-optimal tracker whose two natural
formulations were proven equivalent and whose divergence regimes were mapped; a
demonstration that the same estimator generalizes to monocular real-world
operation, provided its pose uncertainty is propagated honestly; and a detection
front-end, trained on a purpose-built synthetic dataset, whose dominant
accuracy lever and architectural trade-offs were laid bare. The unifying lesson
is that a recursive estimator, instrumented with a faithful model of where its
uncertainty originates, is what carries the \emph{Sense} stage across sensing
modalities, targets, and environments. Bridging the remaining gap, from
stage-wise validation to an integrated, embedded, real-time system flying against
real obstacles, is the work that turns this perception pipeline into a deployable
component of urban air mobility.
