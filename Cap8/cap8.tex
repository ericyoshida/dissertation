This dissertation addressed the perception (\emph{Sense}) layer of urban UAV
detect-and-avoid: detecting and tracking obstacles in three dimensions, in real
time, from a platform that is itself moving. Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}
isolated three properties that make this problem hard, namely an observer in
motion, depth that is uncertain and range-dependent, and a scarcity of annotated
three-dimensional aerial data, and the work confronted all three through a single
artifact: the modular camera--point-cloud pipeline of
Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}, in which a CNN detector seeds a viewing frustum, a
point-cloud network lifts each detection into a metric box, and a recursive
Bayesian filter turns the stream of detections into calibrated tracks. The three
self-contained studies of Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking}--\ref{cha:airsim} are three
contributions to that one pipeline. This closing
chapter revisits what each study established, draws out the threads that bind
them into one system, states plainly what remains unsolved, and sets the agenda
for an integrated onboard capability.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

The dissertation's contributions follow the \emph{one vision, three
contributions} structure: a systematic positioning of the problem, then three
studies presented here in the order of the pipeline they build, from the
detection front-end that produces the measurements to the tracking stage that
consumes them and its real-world generalization.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Positioning the problem (Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}).}
  A systematic literature review following the PRISMA, Kitchenham, and Wohlin
  protocols screened $183$ unique records and synthesized $173$ studies. Its
  centerpiece, the uniqueness matrix, established that of the nineteen closest
  adjacent works \emph{none} simultaneously targets a UAV, employs a
  frustum-to-PointNet detector with camera--LiDAR fusion, and terminates in an
  integrated tracker for urban sense-and-avoid. The review thereby substantiated
  the gap that the dissertation fills, and explained
  its timing through the recent maturation of light UAV-grade LiDAR, the absence of
  a real aerial 3D benchmark, and the non-trivial domain shift from ground to air.

  \item \textbf{The detection front-end and its data (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}).}
  The two stages that feed the tracker were designed, trained, and evaluated on a
  purpose-built synthetic aerial dataset. A pixel-exact synthetic-composition
  scheme circumvented the simulator's unreliable annotation interfaces and trained
  a 2D detector to $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}=0.994$, while a geometric construction
  supplied trustworthy 3D supervision. The study's principal empirical finding is
  that \emph{foreground extraction, not the box regressor, governs frustum
  localization}: removing the background that dominates the point-cloud
  normalization with a depth-band crop (optionally refined by a learned
  segmentation on top of it) took the 3D error from ${\approx}3$~m to
  ${\approx}1$~m. The chapter also delivered the
  first empirical comparison of the two-stage frustum design against the
  single-stage voxel paradigm on aerial data, exposing a clean recall--precision
  trade-off: the frustum pipeline is precise (AMOTA $0.81$, precision $0.90$) but
  inherits its 2D detector's recall ceiling (${\approx}0.49$), whereas a
  learned-feature voxel detector recalls far more targets ($0.80$) and localizes
  them best ($0.87$~m RMSE, $0.88$~m on the twelve sequences held out from its own
  training) at the cost of a lower AMOTA, with a late fusion
  recovering much of both. Crucially, the localization error is essentially flat in
  both of the variables that could have degraded it: it does not grow with range,
  and it does not drift along a sequence --- over the $5{,}359$ matched pairs of
  the campaign the binned median stays within $0.84$--$1.00$~m from the first
  frame to the last (Section~\ref{sec:results-temporal}). Together these mirror
  the range-bounded uncertainty of the Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} filter and
  confirm that detector and tracker are matched by design.

  \item \textbf{The tracking stage and the reference-frame question
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}).}
  The tracking filter was formulated for a moving platform observing a
  target through range--bearing measurements, in two natural variants: one carrying
  the object state in the global inertial frame, the other in the local sensor
  frame. The central result is an \emph{equivalence theorem}: under a known
  platform pose and rotation-invariant (isotropic) process noise, the two
  formulations are mathematically identical, a claim proven analytically and
  verified numerically to $1.7\times10^{-13}$~m, with both filters attaining the
  same RMSE of $0.228$~m. This corrects the framing of the earlier conference
  report \cite{delima2025fusion}: the apparent superiority of the global frame was
  an artifact of a local filter that omitted the inter-frame state reorientation,
  not an intrinsic advantage. The chapter further characterized precisely the
  regimes where a genuine difference arises, namely anisotropic process noise
  (where the frame that diagonalizes $\bm{Q}$ wins, a $41\%$ RMSE difference under
  strong directional noise and platform yaw) and, most consequentially, an
  uncertain platform pose. Benchmarking against the posterior Cram\'er--Rao bound
  confirmed the filter to be near-optimal, with an estimation error that grows
  gracefully with range, exactly the behavior a collision-avoidance system
  requires.

  \item \textbf{Generality of the framework on real data
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}).}
  The same constant-velocity EKF was then taken out of simulation and onto a real
  quadrotor at TU Berlin, with two of the simulation's assumptions removed at once:
  the metric range--bearing sensor became a single monocular camera (depth
  recovered through a ground-plane constraint), and the platform pose became an
  on-board estimate rather than a known quantity. Tracking a freely walking person
  during flights with full $360^\circ$ yaw rotations, the system attained a median
  ground-plane error of $1.43$~m over a ${\sim}12$~m arena from monocular cues alone
  and without GNSS, against a LiDAR-referenced ground truth and with no post-hoc
  alignment; the median rises to $1.75$~m if the intervals in which the target
  left the field of view, and the detector fired on background structure, are
  included --- those intervals being identified by a purely geometric visibility
  test rather than by a tuned threshold. The unfiltered per-frame ground-plane lift is accurate to $1.05$~m,
  so the filter preserves rather than degrades the accuracy of the geometry. More important than the accuracy was the consistency result: this is
  exactly the uncertain-pose regime that Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} predicted would
  break the frame equivalence, and propagating the six-degree-of-freedom camera-pose
  covariance into the innovation through an extrinsic Jacobian proved
  \emph{necessary}: without it the filter is overconfident. An ablation showed that omitting this term leaves
  the filter overconfident by four orders of magnitude (mean NIS $3.4\times10^{4}$
  against the consistent value of $2$), whereas including it brings the filter back
  to the consistent regime, erring on the conservative side. The study thus demonstrated both the generality of the estimator
  across sensing modalities and the condition under which that generality holds: an
  honest model of where the uncertainty comes from.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{Synthesis.}
Read together, the studies make a single claim about \emph{one} system. The
common thread is calibrated uncertainty: every stage was designed to report not
only where an obstacle is but how trustworthy that estimate is, because the
downstream avoidance logic reasons about risk. For this reason the pipeline
terminates in a Bayesian filter rather than a regressor,
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} analyzed the filter's \emph{consistency} against the
Cram\'er--Rao bound in addition to its accuracy, and the central real-world
result of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} is a consistency result. The second thread
is geometric, interpretable fusion: the image localizes the search and the point
cloud supplies the depth, keeping each modality independently trainable and each
stage independently validatable, which allowed the tracker to be
proven correct in isolation, the detector to be evaluated on its own metrics, and
the same estimator to serve both a 3D LiDAR-fused obstacle and a monocular person.
Finally, the boundary that
Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:monocular} share, known versus uncertain
platform pose, marks where the framework moves from its equivalent to its
divergent regime, so accounting for the pose-estimate covariance is important.

\section{Limitations}

The contributions are best understood alongside an honest account of what the
work does not yet establish.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The pipeline is validated stage-wise, not yet end-to-end on
  hardware.} Each stage was exercised against its own ground truth, by design of
  the modular architecture, but no single experiment runs the full
  camera--point-cloud chain on a real UAV against real three-dimensional
  obstacles. The obstacle tracker was validated in simulation
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}), the detection front-end in simulation
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}), and only the monocular tracker on real flights
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}).
  \item \textbf{The detection front-end is trained and tested purely in
  simulation.} The AirSim study sidesteps the data-scarcity obstacle but inherits
  a sim-to-real gap: the detectors have not been confronted with the noise,
  lighting, and appearance statistics of real aerial footage, so their reported
  accuracy is an upper bound on real-world performance.
  \item \textbf{The simulated point cloud is an idealized depth image, not a real
  LiDAR.} As set out in Section~\ref{sec:sensor_caveat}, the cloud that feeds the
  three-dimensional stage in Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} is produced by AirSim's
  planar-depth camera: dense, noiseless, free of dropouts, and independent of
  surface material, incidence angle, and weather. A real airborne LiDAR is sparse,
  angularly quantized, noisy, prone to dropouts on dark or specular surfaces,
  degraded by rain and fog, and subject to occlusion and motion-distortion
  geometries that a globally shuttered depth image does not have. The localization
  accuracies of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} should therefore be read as an upper
  bound obtained under uniformly favorable sensing, and the frustum-versus-voxel
  ranking as one established under those same favorable conditions; the two
  paradigms could plausibly reorder under sparse returns, since the voxel design
  depends more heavily on having enough points on the target.
  \item \textbf{Tracking is delivered, but trajectory prediction is not.} The
  estimator outputs a position, a velocity, and a covariance, from which a future
  position can be extrapolated through the constant-velocity model, and that is
  what the avoidance layer would use. It is nevertheless an extrapolation of
  kinematics, not a prediction of behavior: it does not model the obstacle's
  intent, its maneuver repertoire, or the structure of the airspace, and its
  uncertainty envelope therefore widens conservatively with the horizon. Since
  avoidance is fundamentally a decision about the \emph{future} conflict geometry
  and not about the present position, this is a real gap between what the
  perception layer delivers and what a complete detect-and-avoid function needs.
  \item \textbf{No comparison against an external benchmark.} The detection and
  tracking results of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} are internally comparative: the
  architectures are compared against each other, on a purpose-built dataset, under
  an identical protocol. They are not placed against any published method on any
  public benchmark, because no aerial three-dimensional benchmark with the
  required annotations was available when the campaign was run and because the
  synthetic dataset is this work's own. The consequence is that the reported
  numbers establish a ranking among the alternatives implemented here but do not
  situate the pipeline within the wider literature in absolute terms.
  \item \textbf{Recall, not precision, limits the two-stage design.} The frustum
  pipeline's recall falls to $0.31$--$0.58$ under tree clutter and fog, inherited
  almost entirely from the 2D stage. In a collision-avoidance application a missed
  obstacle is the most dangerous failure, so this ceiling is a genuine
  limitation, only partially relieved by the voxel and fusion variants.
  \item \textbf{The monocular tracker rests on a ground-plane assumption.} The
  single-camera lift trades the unobservable depth for a known target height; any
  violation of that assumption, or any error in the foot-point detection that
  stands in for ground contact, maps directly to a planar error that grows with
  range. The largest errors concentrate during the platform's most aggressive
  rotations, where the estimated pose is least accurate. The study also rests on a
  single scene and a single target class.
  \item \textbf{The multi-object layer is not analyzed with the rigor of the
  single-target filter.} The data association and track lifecycle of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} are a heuristic, SORT-style layer; the
  Cram\'er--Rao and consistency analysis of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} applies to
  the per-track filter, not to the association problem; identity performance
  (IDF1) was measured for the frustum pipeline only and not for the voxel
  detectors.
  \item \textbf{Pose-uncertainty propagation has not been carried into the 3D
  pipeline.} The extrinsic-aware innovation covariance of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} was derived for the monocular projective
  measurement. The 3D detection-fed tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}, by
  contrast, treats the platform pose as \emph{known} and does not yet propagate
  the pose-estimate covariance the way the Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} monocular
  tracker does; doing so is left as future work.
  \item \textbf{No real-time, embedded demonstration.} All experiments were run on
  the laptop-class machine described in Section~\ref{sec:compute}, an Intel
  Core~i9-13900HX with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~4080 laptop GPU. On that hardware the recursive filter
  is real-time by a wide margin and the 2D detector meets the frame rate at
  roughly $5$~ms per frame, but the composed pipeline does not: network inference
  in the real-flight study lags the live stream by two to three seconds, and the
  full three-dimensional chain of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} is heavier still, so
  all inference was executed offline. End-to-end latency was never benchmarked,
  and no experiment was run on hardware representative of an onboard computer, so
  the compute budget that an embedded sense-and-avoid system imposes
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}) has not yet been met or even measured.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work}

These limitations chart a concrete agenda, ordered roughly from the most
immediate to the most ambitious.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Closing the sim-to-real gap.} The most direct next step is to
  validate the Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} detectors on real aerial footage, exactly
  as the monocular tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} was validated on real
  flights. Domain randomization and domain adaptation during synthetic training,
  together with the collection or annotation of a modest real aerial 3D set, are
  the natural means; the foreground-extraction finding suggests that robustifying
  the segmentation to real clutter is where the effort should concentrate.
  \item \textbf{End-to-end integration on a single platform.} The stages should be
  composed into one system running the full
  2D~$\rightarrow$~frustum~$\rightarrow$~3D~$\rightarrow$~EKF chain on a real UAV,
  closing the loop that the modular validation deliberately left open. A first
  integration can run on a companion computer; deployment on constrained onboard
  hardware within a real-time budget, with measured computation times, is a
  subsequent step. The recall ceiling motivates fielding the late
  frustum--voxel fusion rather than the frustum pipeline alone.
  \item \textbf{Tighter detector--tracker coupling.} The detector's own
  uncertainty should feed the filter's measurement covariance $\bm{R}$ rather than
  a fixed model, and the heuristic association should give way to an
  uncertainty-aware, possibly learned, scheme that receives the rigorous treatment
  the single-target filter already enjoys.
  \item \textbf{Pose uncertainty in the full 3D pipeline.} The pose-covariance
  propagation proven necessary in Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} should be carried
  into the spherical-measurement tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}, instantiating
  the uncertain-pose divergent regime of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} for the 3D
  case and yielding a tracker that is consistent under the real navigation
  solution rather than an assumed-known pose. Two concrete steps precede it. The
  extrinsic Jacobian must be re-derived for the spherical measurement, composing
  the spherical Jacobian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Jsph} with the $SE(3)$ perturbation
  block of Eq.~\eqref{eq:se3_pert} in place of the projection Jacobian; and the
  navigation subsystem must be made to \emph{publish} its covariance, which is the
  practical obstacle that forced the constant, isotropic $\bm{\Sigma}$ of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}. The second is the more important of the two: a
  time-varying $\bm{\Sigma}_k$ would let the filter widen its gate precisely
  during the aggressive maneuvers in which the pose is least trustworthy, which a
  single tuned scalar cannot do.

  \item \textbf{Trajectory prediction, and metrics that reward it.} Tracking is
  not sufficient for avoidance. An evasive maneuver takes time to plan and to fly,
  so the decision layer needs the obstacle's \emph{future} position over a horizon
  of seconds, with an honest uncertainty envelope, and not merely its present
  state. Extrapolating the constant-velocity model gives a first, kinematic
  answer, and it is what the present estimator affords; a behavior-aware predictor,
  learned or maneuver-model-based, would be the substantive extension, and the
  detection front-end is arguably better placed than the filter to supply the
  contextual cues (object class, attitude, apparent intent) such a predictor needs.
  This change of objective implies a change of metrics. Neither RMSE nor MOTA
  measures whether a system would have avoided a collision, since both score the
  \emph{current} estimate; the appropriate figures of merit are predictive and
  risk-oriented, such as average and final displacement error at a fixed horizon,
  time-to-conflict or time-to-closest-approach error, and the rate of missed or
  spurious conflict alerts. Adopting them would also make the comparison between
  the detection paradigms of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} conclusive for the intended
  task, which the present detection-and-tracking metrics can only approximate.

  \item \textbf{External benchmarks and comparison against published methods.} The
  architectural comparison of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} is internally consistent but
  self-contained. Before those results are submitted for publication they should be
  anchored to the wider literature in two ways: by evaluating the same pipeline on
  a public aerial three-dimensional benchmark, of which UAV3D
  \cite{ye2024uav3d} is currently the only large-scale option, and by running the
  same pipeline end to end on the driving benchmarks the frustum and voxel families
  were originally validated on, KITTI \cite{geiger2012kitti} and nuScenes
  \cite{caesar2020nuscenes}, so that a like-for-like comparison becomes possible.
  Merely quoting their published numbers alongside the ones reported here would not
  provide it, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. A published
  reference point obtained this way is what turns a ranking among one's own
  variants into a contribution a reviewer can situate.

  \item \textbf{Realistic range sensing.} The idealized depth camera of
  Section~\ref{sec:sensor_caveat} should give way to a validated LiDAR model, and
  ultimately to real returns from one of the light UAV-grade units now available,
  such as the Livox Mid-360 already used in the survey of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} \cite{livox_mid360}. Sparsity, dropouts, and
  incidence-angle effects are exactly the conditions under which the
  frustum-versus-voxel trade-off could reorder, since the voxel design depends on
  having enough returns on the target, so this is not merely a fidelity exercise
  but a test of the chapter's central architectural conclusion.

  \item \textbf{Multiple object classes.} Both studies track a single class, a
  quadrotor in simulation and a person in the real flights. Urban airspace is not
  so obliging: birds, fixed-wing aircraft, larger eVTOLs, and ground vehicles
  differ in size, in dynamics, and in the motion model that best describes them.
  Extending the front-end to a multi-class detector and the tracker to
  class-conditioned motion models, possibly through an interacting-multiple-model
  filter, is a natural next step, and it also changes the association problem,
  since class identity is an additional and highly discriminative association cue.
  \item \textbf{A range-adaptive, mode-switching tracker.} The two tracking
  studies are complementary in range. An active depth or LiDAR sensor gives a
  metric range--bearing measurement at close range (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}),
  but its ranging saturates and returns nothing once the obstacle is far enough
  away or the return is too weak; at that point only the camera still resolves the
  target, and the monocular, ground-constrained estimator of
  Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} takes over. A natural integration is therefore a
  single tracker that \emph{switches modes} with range (Figure~\ref{fig:range_adaptive}),
  fusing the metric 3D
  detection while the depth sensor is in range and falling back to the
  bearing-only monocular update beyond it, so that the obstacle is tracked
  continuously across the whole approach. This unifies the two studies into one
  estimator and is, in our view, the most promising way to combine them.
  \item \textbf{Collaborative and joint multi-UAV perception.} Because the tracker
  consumes an abstract measurement with a calibrated covariance, tracks estimated
  by several platforms can in principle be fused into a shared situational
  picture, extending the single-observer awareness of this work to a cooperative
  one. Two variants are worth distinguishing. The simpler is \emph{cooperative
  tracking}, in which each aircraft runs its own filter and only the resulting
  tracks are fused; the harder, and the more interesting, is \emph{joint
  cooperative pose estimation and tracking}, in which the common targets are also
  used to tie the platforms' own pose estimates together, so that an aircraft with
  a degraded navigation solution is corrected by what its neighbors see. The
  latter directly relaxes the assumption that Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} isolates
  and that Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} works around, and it would replace the
  block-diagonal treatment of platform and target uncertainty by a genuinely
  coupled one. Whether it is appropriate for sense-and-avoid is a design question
  rather than a settled one, since it introduces a dependence on the
  communication link and a correlated-error problem that a
  certification argument must address. The same caution applies to the related
  idea of \emph{centralized} filtering, in which small aircraft stream their
  imagery to one powerful ground computer in order to escape their onboard compute
  budget: attractive on throughput grounds, it concentrates the perception function
  in a single point of failure, which is difficult to defend for a
  collision-avoidance function and unacceptable in contested or
  communication-denied operation.
  \item \textbf{Closing the loop to \emph{Avoid}.} The calibrated tracks this
  pipeline emits were designed for a risk-aware avoidance layer; coupling the
  perception output to trajectory planning and control would complete the
  detect-and-avoid capability of which this dissertation built the \emph{Sense}
  half.
\end{itemize}

\section{Concluding Remarks}

Safe autonomous flight in the urban canyon will be certified, ultimately, on the
trustworthiness of the aircraft's situational awareness, and trustworthiness
means not only accurate estimates but estimates that know how uncertain they are.
This dissertation contributed the perception layer of that capability as a single
modular camera--point-cloud pipeline and validated its components from three
complementary angles: a rigorous, near-optimal tracker whose two natural
formulations were proven equivalent and whose divergence regimes were mapped; a
demonstration that the same estimator generalizes to monocular real-world
operation, provided its pose uncertainty is propagated honestly; and a detection
front-end, trained on a purpose-built synthetic dataset, whose dominant
accuracy lever and architectural trade-offs were laid bare. The unifying lesson
is that a recursive estimator, instrumented with a faithful model of where its
uncertainty originates, carries the \emph{Sense} stage across sensing
modalities, targets, and environments. Bridging the remaining gap, from
stage-wise validation to an integrated, embedded, real-time system flying against
real obstacles, is the work that turns this perception pipeline into a deployable
component of urban air mobility.
