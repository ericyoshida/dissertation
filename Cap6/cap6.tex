This chapter takes the estimation framework of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} out of
simulation and onto a real aerial platform. The obstacle tracker developed there
assumed a metric range--bearing sensor and a \emph{known} platform pose, and both
assumptions are relaxed here at once. The sensor is now a single calibrated
camera, with no depth and no LiDAR on the target, and the platform pose is
\emph{estimated} on board and therefore uncertain. The target is a walking person
rather than a generic obstacle. The study was carried out during a three-month
research stay with the UBADRON team of the Chair of Flight Guidance and Air
Transport at the Technische Universit\"at Berlin (TU~Berlin), led by
Prof.~Maarten Uijt de Haag, using a custom quadrotor and real flight data. The
collaboration arose after the FUSION~2025 tracker was presented at ITA: the
UBADRON team, which competes in the SPRIND ``Fully Autonomous Flight'' Challenge
with a GNSS-denied UAV that must locate people on the terrain for
search-and-rescue, already had a person detector but needed a tracker, and so the
two groups joined forces. It is the
empirical counterpart of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}, and it operates in the
regime where the global and local formulations of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} stop
being equivalent: when the platform pose is uncertain, its covariance must be
propagated into the filter, and doing so correctly is the central methodological
contribution of this chapter.

\section{Overview and Objectives}
\label{sec:mono_overview}

The guiding question is one of \emph{generality}: does the recursive-filter
tracking stage of the pipeline (Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}) survive the
transition from a controlled, range-sensing simulation to a monocular,
real-world deployment? Answering it requires three adaptations of the
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} estimator, each addressing one property that the
simulation took for granted.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{From range--bearing to bearing-only sensing.} A monocular camera
  measures a pixel, not a range, and depth is unobservable from a single view. The
  spherical measurement model of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} is replaced by the
  nonlinear pinhole projection of Chapter~\ref{cha:background}, and the lost
  depth dimension is recovered through a \emph{ground-plane} constraint: the
  target is a person standing on a surface of known height, which closes the
  projective ambiguity.
  \item \textbf{From known to uncertain platform pose.} The pose is supplied by
  the onboard navigation system and is itself an estimate. Its
  six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) uncertainty is propagated into the innovation
  covariance through an \emph{extrinsic Jacobian}, so that the filter inflates
  its gate when the platform pose is least trustworthy, during aggressive
  maneuvers.
  \item \textbf{From a generic obstacle to a learned person detector.} The 2D
  measurement is produced by a CNN person detector. Since the operating scene
  differs from any
  single public benchmark, the detector is trained on a \emph{union} of aerial
  person-detection datasets spanning urban, rural, near, and far viewpoints, so
  that it generalizes to the deployment environment.
\end{enumerate}

The objective of the chapter is therefore twofold: to formulate a monocular,
pose-uncertainty-aware EKF for person tracking from a moving UAV, and to validate
it on real flights against an independently surveyed, LiDAR-referenced ground
truth. The remainder follows the self-contained structure of the study
chapters. Section~\ref{sec:mono_method} develops the methodology, covering the
detector, the measurement model, the ground-plane initialization, the
extrinsic-aware innovation covariance, the temporal synchronization, and the
experimental platform; Section~\ref{sec:mono_results} reports the experimental
protocol and results; and Section~\ref{sec:mono_discussion} connects the findings
back to the equivalence theorem of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:mono_method}

The monocular tracker is organized as two branches that meet at the filter, shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:mono_pipeline}. A \emph{vision} branch turns each frame into a
metric measurement: the image is undistorted, a person detector
(Section~\ref{sec:mono_detector}) localizes the target, and its foot pixel is
extracted (Section~\ref{sec:mono_meas}). A \emph{navigation} branch supplies the
platform pose and its uncertainty, temporally aligned to the frame
(Section~\ref{sec:mono_sync}). The two meet in the ground-plane geometry: the
first detection is lifted to a metric position by ray--plane intersection
(Section~\ref{sec:mono_groundplane}), and every subsequent detection updates an
EKF whose innovation covariance is inflated by the propagated pose uncertainty
(Section~\ref{sec:mono_extrinsic}). The following subsections develop each block
in turn.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap6/pipeline_monocular.pdf}
  \caption{The monocular person-tracking pipeline. The vision branch (top)
  produces the foot-pixel measurement; the navigation branch (bottom) produces
  the time-synchronized camera pose and its covariance $\bm{\Sigma}$. They meet at
  the ground-plane initialization and the extrinsic-aware EKF update, whose
  innovation covariance
  $\bm{S}=\bm{H}\bm{P}\bm{H}^{\top}+\bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\top}+\bm{R}_{\mathrm{cam}}$
  carries the pose uncertainty into the filter.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Cross-Domain Training of the Person Detector}
\label{sec:mono_detector}

The first stage of the monocular pipeline is the 2D person detector that supplies
the filter's measurements. The deployment scene, an indoor sports hall imaged
from a low-altitude quadrotor, is not represented by any single public benchmark,
since aerial person-detection datasets are each specialized to one regime of
altitude, viewpoint, and background. Training on any one of them in isolation
yields a detector that overfits that regime and degrades under the domain shift
of a new environment.

To obtain a detector that generalizes, we trained a modern one-stage YOLO
detector (Chapter~\ref{cha:background}; the original single-stage idea is due to
\citen{redmon2016yolo}, and we use the current Ultralytics
implementation~\cite{ultralytics2024yolo11}) on the \emph{union} of three
complementary aerial datasets, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:datasets}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{VisDrone} \cite{zhu2021visdrone} is a large-scale urban
  drone-imagery benchmark with dense pedestrians and vehicles seen from oblique,
  low-to-medium altitudes (Figure~\ref{fig:datasets}a). It contributes
  \emph{urban} clutter and crowded, small-object scenes.
  \item \textbf{SARD} \cite{sambolek2021sard} is a search-and-rescue dataset of
  actors emulating lost or injured persons in rural terrain, captured at close
  range and oblique angles (Figure~\ref{fig:datasets}b). It contributes
  \emph{near-range, non-canonical body poses} (crouching, lying) against natural
  backgrounds.
  \item \textbf{HERIDAL} \cite{bozic2019heridal} is a wilderness
  search-and-rescue dataset acquired at high altitude with a near-nadir
  viewpoint, in which persons appear as very small, low-contrast targets over
  heterogeneous terrain (Figure~\ref{fig:datasets}c). It contributes the
  \emph{far-range, tiny-target} regime.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap6/datasets_montage.pdf}
  \caption{The three aerial person-detection datasets combined to train the
  detector. Their complementary regimes (urban and oblique in VisDrone,
  rural and near-range in SARD, wilderness and high-altitude-nadir in HERIDAL)
  span the viewpoints, scales, and backgrounds that no single benchmark provides,
  so that the trained detector generalizes to the unseen TU Berlin sports hall
  where the real flights were recorded.}
  \label{fig:datasets}
\end{figure}

The three datasets were chosen to be \emph{orthogonal} along the axes that drive
aerial domain shift: altitude (near to far), viewpoint (oblique to nadir), and
environment (built to natural). Their union therefore covers the interior of that
space instead of a single corner of it. Training on the combined corpus yields a
detector whose decision boundary is shaped by the full range of scales and
appearances, and this generalization lets the network, trained entirely on
external data, detect the person reliably in the TU Berlin sports hall, a scene
present in none of the training sets. The three datasets
are all \emph{outdoor}, whereas the deployment scene is an \emph{indoor} sports
hall; the cross-domain training is precisely what bridges this gap, since no
indoor aerial person-detection benchmark of comparable scale exists, and the
person's appearance (an upright human seen from a low-altitude oblique view) is
shared across the two settings even though the background is not. This trained
network is the detector used throughout the tracking experiments of this chapter;
for each frame it outputs the set of person bounding boxes from which the filter's
measurement is extracted.

The combined corpus comprises $14{,}783$ labeled aerial images of a single
\emph{person} class. A YOLOv11-small detector was trained on it
(at $1280$~px for $50$ epochs; full settings in Appendix~\ref{ap:train_hyper}),
and on a held-out validation split it reaches a mean average precision of
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50}=0.754$ and $\mathrm{mAP}_{50\text{--}95}=0.389$, with a
precision of $0.795$ and a recall of $0.669$ at its operating point. These are
solid numbers for a single small detector required to span urban, rural, and
wilderness aerial imagery at once: the cross-domain training trades a few points
of peak accuracy on any single domain for the robustness that lets the same
network, trained entirely on outdoor public data, generalize to the unseen indoor
sports hall. The recall of $0.67$ also sets context for the tracking results: the
target is missed in roughly a third of frames, which is precisely the regime the
recursive filter and its re-initialization logic (Section~\ref{sec:mono_groundplane})
are designed to bridge.

\subsection{Monocular Measurement Model and Plumb-Bob Undistortion}
\label{sec:mono_meas}

The filter state is the target's planar position and velocity on the ground
plane,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{x}_k = \begin{bmatrix} x_k & y_k & \dot{x}_k & \dot{y}_k \end{bmatrix}^{\!\top},
  \label{eq:mono_state}
\end{equation}
expressed in the world frame $\mathcal{W}$ of Chapter~\ref{cha:background}; the
tracker thus operates in the global-frame formulation adopted in
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}. The target is propagated with the constant-velocity,
white-acceleration model \eqref{eq:cv}--\eqref{eq:cwna}: with sampling interval
$\Delta t$ and acceleration intensity $\sigma_a$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \bm{x}_{k} = \bm{F}\,\bm{x}_{k-1} + \bm{w}_{k-1},\qquad
  \bm{F} = \begin{bmatrix} \bm{I}_2 & \Delta t\,\bm{I}_2 \\ \bm{0} & \bm{I}_2 \end{bmatrix},\\[4pt]
  \bm{Q} = \bm{G}\,\sigma_a^2\bm{I}_2\,\bm{G}^{\!\top},\qquad
  \bm{G} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{\Delta t^2}{2}\bm{I}_2 \\ \Delta t\,\bm{I}_2 \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:mono_process}
\end{equation}

The one change of substance relative
to Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} is the measurement: a camera reports the
\emph{pixel} projection of the target, not its range and bearing.

Let $\bm{T}_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{O},k}=(\bm{R}^{wo}_k,\bm{t}^{wo}_k)$ be the
rigid transform from world to optical frame at time $k$, composed from the
platform pose and the fixed camera mounting (Section~\ref{sec:mono_platform}). A
world point $\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}$ maps to the optical frame as
$\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}=\bm{R}^{wo}_k\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}+\bm{t}^{wo}_k$ and projects
to the normalized pixel through the pinhole model \eqref{eq:pinhole},
\begin{equation}
  \bm{h}(\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}) = \pi\!\big(\bm{K}\,\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}\big)
  = \begin{bmatrix} f_x\,X^{\mathcal{O}}/Z^{\mathcal{O}} + c_x \\[2pt]
                    f_y\,Y^{\mathcal{O}}/Z^{\mathcal{O}} + c_y \end{bmatrix}.
  \label{eq:mono_h}
\end{equation}
The intrinsics $\bm{K}$ and the radial and tangential (plumb-bob) distortion
coefficients are obtained by an offline checkerboard calibration. Because the measurement model \eqref{eq:mono_h} is the
\emph{distortion-free} pinhole map, every detected pixel is first undistorted: the
plumb-bob model is inverted by fixed-point iteration on the normalized
coordinates, recovering the ideal pinhole pixel that \eqref{eq:mono_h} predicts.
All measurements entering the filter are thus expressed in the rectified image,
which keeps the measurement equation a pure projection.

From each confirmed person detection, the measurement is taken as the mid-bottom
of the bounding box,
$\bm{z}_k=[\tfrac{1}{2}(x_1+x_2),\,y_2]^{\top}$, where $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$
are the top-left and bottom-right box corners, so that $\tfrac{1}{2}(x_1+x_2)$ is
the horizontal midpoint and $y_2$ the bottom edge. This point is used as a
\emph{heuristic} estimate of the pixel at which the person contacts the ground:
it is not guaranteed to coincide with the true contact point (a raised foot, a
leaning posture, or a clipped detection displaces it), but it is the pixel that
the ground-plane constraint (next subsection) maps to a metric position. The
bounding-box centroid, by contrast, floats with the target's apparent height and
does not lie on the plane. Figure~\ref{fig:footpoint} illustrates the geometry.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Cap6/footpoint_geometry.pdf}
  \caption{Ground-plane lift of the foot pixel. The mid-bottom of the detection
  box (the foot pixel) defines a viewing ray $\bm{r}^{\mathcal{W}}$ from the
  camera center $\bm{C}^{\mathcal{W}}$; intersecting that ray with the ground
  plane $\mathcal{P}=\{Z^{\mathcal{W}}=z_{\mathrm{obj}}\}$ yields the metric
  position $\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}_0$ of the target's ground contact. The foot pixel
  is a heuristic stand-in for the true contact point.}
  \label{fig:footpoint}
\end{figure}

The accuracy of this heuristic is bounded by the quality of the detector's box.
Even when the person is detected well, the box mid-bottom does not land exactly on
the ground contact: mid-stride the box encloses both the planted and the trailing
foot, and a slightly loose or tight box shifts its lower edge, so the foot pixel
is offset from the true contact by a few tens of pixels.
Figure~\ref{fig:footpoint_err} shows two frames of the (correctly detected)
walker in which this offset is $26$--$32$~px. Because the ray--plane lift turns a
vertical pixel offset into a ground-range error that grows with distance, this is
a real, if modest, error source, distinct from the platform-pose uncertainty; it
sets a floor on the achievable accuracy even with a perfect navigation solution.
This foot-point geometry, evaluated frame by frame against the ground truth, is in
fact what governs the \emph{typical} accuracy of the tracker
(Section~\ref{sec:mono_accuracy}); the larger excursions there are a separate
phenomenon, dominated by detector false positives during the fast-yaw intervals
when the target leaves the field of view.

\subsection{Ground-Plane Constraint and Ray--Plane Initialization}
\label{sec:mono_groundplane}

A single bearing does not determine a 3D position, since the pre-image of a pixel
under \eqref{eq:mono_h} is an entire ray. The missing constraint is supplied by
the geometry of the task. A person stands on a surface of known height, so the
target's vertical coordinate in the world frame is fixed at a known value
$z_{\mathrm{obj}}$, and its state is confined to the horizontal plane
$\mathcal{P}=\{\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}: Z^{\mathcal{W}}=z_{\mathrm{obj}}\}$. This
assumption reduces the state to the planar form \eqref{eq:mono_state}
and makes a single camera sufficient for metric localization. We adopt it
specifically because the platform here carries only a monocular camera: with no
range sensor to supply depth, the ground-plane constraint is what closes the
otherwise underdetermined single-view geometry. Where a depth or LiDAR channel is
available, as in the full pipeline of Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}, this assumption
is unnecessary and is dropped.

The constraint is used directly to initialize a new track. Given the first
undistorted foot pixel $\bm{u}=(u,v)$, the corresponding viewing ray is
constructed in the optical frame from the inverse intrinsics,
$\bm{d}^{\mathcal{O}}=[(u-c_x)/f_x,\;(v-c_y)/f_y,\;1]^{\top}$, rotated into the
world frame, and intersected with $\mathcal{P}$. With camera center
$\bm{C}^{\mathcal{W}}=-\bm{R}^{wo\top}_k\bm{t}^{wo}_k$ and ray direction
$\bm{r}^{\mathcal{W}}=\bm{R}^{wo\top}_k\bm{d}^{\mathcal{O}}$, the intersection is
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^\star = \frac{z_{\mathrm{obj}} - C^{\mathcal{W}}_z}{r^{\mathcal{W}}_z},
  \qquad
  \bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}_0 = \bm{C}^{\mathcal{W}} + \lambda^\star\,\bm{r}^{\mathcal{W}},
  \label{eq:rayplane}
\end{equation}
defined whenever $r^{\mathcal{W}}_z\neq 0$ and $\lambda^\star>0$ (the target is in
front of the camera). The planar components of $\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}}_0$ initialize
the position state and the velocity is set to zero, with a large initial
covariance so that the first measurements dominate and the filter converges
quickly. Subsequent frames are handled by the standard EKF predict--update
recursion rather than by re-intersection, so that the temporal model
\eqref{eq:mono_process} smooths the inevitably noisy single-frame lift.

A practical caveat governs how long the recursion may coast on prediction alone.
The detector does not fire on every frame: during the platform's full-circle yaw
sweeps the target repeatedly leaves and re-enters the field of view, so the
measurement stream contains long gaps. Propagating the constant-velocity model
\eqref{eq:mono_process} unchecked across such a gap is unsafe, because any
nonzero velocity estimate is extrapolated for the whole gap and the position
diverges. We therefore bound the open-loop horizon: if no detection has been
associated for longer than a hold time $\tau_{\mathrm{gap}}$, the track is
\emph{re-initialized} by ray--plane intersection \eqref{eq:rayplane} on the next
detection instead of being predicted forward, which discards the stale velocity
and re-anchors the state to the current measurement. This re-initialization is
what keeps the ground-plane lift, rather than the motion model, in control of the
estimate when the target reappears after a yaw sweep.

\subsection{Extrinsic-Aware Innovation Covariance}
\label{sec:mono_extrinsic}

The measurement model \eqref{eq:mono_h} depends not only on the target state but
also on the platform pose, through $\bm{T}_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{O},k}$. In
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} the pose was assumed known and this dependence was
deterministic; on the real platform the pose is an \emph{estimate} delivered by
the navigation system, and its error feeds directly into the predicted pixel. A
filter that ignores this and treats the pose as exact underestimates its own
innovation covariance and so becomes overconfident, and it diverges when the pose
error is largest, that is, during aggressive platform motion. This is the
``uncertain platform pose'' regime identified in Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} as
the setting where the global and local formulations cease to be equivalent.

The remedy is to propagate the pose uncertainty into the innovation covariance.
The derivation is given here in full, since the final expression
\eqref{eq:Smono} is the methodological core of this chapter and its dimensions
and approximations are easy to lose sight of. It proceeds in four steps: fix the
geometric chain and its dimensions; differentiate it with respect to the
\emph{state}; differentiate it with respect to the \emph{pose}; and combine the
two, with the sensor noise, into the innovation covariance.

\paragraph*{Step 1: the geometric chain.} Lift the planar state to the
three-dimensional point on the ground plane,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}} = \begin{bmatrix} x_k \\ y_k \\ z_{\mathrm{obj}} \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{3},
  \qquad
  \bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}} = \bm{R}^{wo}_k\,\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}} + \bm{t}^{wo}_k
  = \begin{bmatrix} X^{\mathcal{O}} \\ Y^{\mathcal{O}} \\ Z^{\mathcal{O}} \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^{3},
  \label{eq:mono_chain}
\end{equation}
with $\bm{R}^{wo}_k\in SO(3)$ ($3\times3$) and $\bm{t}^{wo}_k\in\mathbb{R}^3$ the
world-to-optical rotation and translation of
$\bm{T}_{\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{O},k}$. The predicted measurement is
$\hat{\bm{z}}_k=\bm{h}(\bm{X}^{\mathcal{W}})=\pi(\bm{K}\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$
from \eqref{eq:mono_h}. The measurement is therefore a function of two distinct
groups of uncertain quantities: the target state $\bm{x}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ and
the camera pose, whose error lives in $\mathbb{R}^{6}$.

\paragraph*{Step 2: the projection Jacobian.} Differentiating
$\pi([X,Y,Z]^{\top})=[X/Z,\,Y/Z]^{\top}$ and scaling by the focal lengths gives
the sensitivity of the pixel to the optical-frame point,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{J}_{\pi} = \frac{\partial \pi(\bm{K}\,\cdot)}{\partial \bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}}
  = \begin{bmatrix}
      f_x/Z^{\mathcal{O}} & 0 & -f_x X^{\mathcal{O}}/(Z^{\mathcal{O}})^2 \\[2pt]
      0 & f_y/Z^{\mathcal{O}} & -f_y Y^{\mathcal{O}}/(Z^{\mathcal{O}})^2
    \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times3},
  \label{eq:Jpi}
\end{equation}
where the third column collects the terms produced by the quotient rule acting on
the depth $Z^{\mathcal{O}}$. Note the $1/Z^{\mathcal{O}}$ and
$1/(Z^{\mathcal{O}})^{2}$ scaling: the same metric displacement subtends fewer
pixels the farther the target is, which is the algebraic root of the
range-dependent degradation reported in Section~\ref{sec:mono_accuracy}. This
$2\times3$ matrix is the common left factor of both Jacobians that follow, since
every uncertain quantity reaches the pixel through $\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}$.

\paragraph*{Step 3: the state Jacobian.} Only the two planar coordinates of the
state are free, the height being fixed at $z_{\mathrm{obj}}$ by the ground-plane
constraint, and the velocities do not appear in \eqref{eq:mono_h} at all.
Differentiating the chain \eqref{eq:mono_chain} with respect to $[x_k,y_k]^{\top}$
gives $\partial\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}/\partial[x_k,y_k]^{\top}=\bm{R}^{wo}_k\bm{E}$,
where $\bm{E}=[\bm{I}_2\;\;\bm{0}_{2\times1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2}$
selects the first two columns of the rotation. Appending the two zero columns that
correspond to the velocity states yields the $2\times4$ measurement Jacobian
\begin{equation}
  \bm{H}_k
  = \underbrace{\bm{J}_{\pi}}_{2\times3}\;
    \underbrace{\bm{R}^{wo}_k}_{3\times3}\;
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_2\\\bm{0}_{1\times2}\end{bmatrix}}_{3\times2}\;
    \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_2 & \bm{0}_{2\times2}\end{bmatrix}}_{2\times4}
  \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{2\times4},
  \label{eq:Hmono}
\end{equation}
the trailing block reflecting that velocities affect the measurement only
indirectly, through the prediction step.

\paragraph*{Step 4: the extrinsic Jacobian.} The pose error is not a vector in the
ordinary sense, since $SE(3)$ is not a vector space; it is represented by the
coordinate vector
$\bm{\xi}=[\bm{\delta\theta}^{\top}\;\bm{\delta t}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$
of an element of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{se}(3)$, as set out in
Section~\ref{sec:pose_perturbations}. The true pose is written multiplicatively as
$\tilde{\bm{T}}=\exp(\bm{\xi}^{\wedge})\bm{T}$, and the derivation rests on
\emph{one} approximation, namely the first-order truncation of the exponential
series \eqref{eq:se3_exp_approx},
$\exp(\bm{\xi}^{\wedge})=\bm{I}_4+\bm{\xi}^{\wedge}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\xi}^{\wedge})^{2}+\cdots\approx\bm{I}_4+\bm{\xi}^{\wedge}$,
whose neglected remainder is $O(\lVert\bm{\xi}\rVert^{2})$. Applying it to
$\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}$ and using $[\bm{a}]_\times\bm{b}=-[\bm{b}]_\times\bm{a}$ to
make the expression linear in the unknown gives \eqref{eq:se3_pert},
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}
  = \big[\,-[\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}]_\times \;\; \bm{I}_3\,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times6}.
  \label{eq:dp_dxi}
\end{equation}
Composing \eqref{eq:dp_dxi} with the projection Jacobian by the chain rule gives
the extrinsic Jacobian of the pixel with respect to the pose,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}
  = \frac{\partial\bm{h}}{\partial\bm{\xi}}
  = \underbrace{\bm{J}_{\pi}}_{2\times3}
    \underbrace{\big[\,-[\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}]_\times \;\; \bm{I}_3\,\big]}_{3\times6}
  \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{2\times6},
  \label{eq:Jext}
\end{equation}
whose rotational block $-\bm{J}_{\pi}[\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}]_\times$
($2\times3$) and translational block $\bm{J}_{\pi}$ ($2\times3$) map,
respectively, an attitude error and a position error of the platform into a pixel
displacement of the target. The two blocks scale differently with geometry, and
this is what makes the term behave sensibly: the translational block falls off as
$1/Z^{\mathcal{O}}$, so a position error of the platform matters less for a
distant target, whereas the rotational block contains
$[\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}]_\times$, whose entries \emph{grow} with the target
distance, so an attitude error matters more. Attitude, not position, is therefore
the dominant pose-error channel at range, which is consistent with the fact that
the observed inconsistency concentrates in the high-yaw-rate intervals
(Section~\ref{sec:mono_consistency}).

\paragraph*{Step 5: the augmented innovation covariance.} The innovation is
$\bm{\nu}_k=\bm{z}_k-\hat{\bm{z}}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$, and to first order it
collects three mutually independent error sources: the error of the predicted
state, with covariance $\bm{P}_{k|k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times4}$, mapped through
$\bm{H}_k$; the error of the camera pose, with covariance
$\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times6}$, mapped through $\bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}$; and
the pixel measurement noise itself,
$\bm{R}_{\mathrm{cam}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{cam}}^2\bm{I}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$.
Writing $\bm{\nu}_k\approx\bm{H}_k\,\delta\bm{x}_{k|k-1}+\bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}\,\bm{\xi}+\bm{v}_k$
and taking the expectation of $\bm{\nu}_k\bm{\nu}_k^{\top}$, the cross terms
vanish by independence and the three quadratic forms add, giving
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\bm{S}_k}_{2\times2}
  = \underbrace{\bm{H}_k\,\bm{P}_{k|k-1}\,\bm{H}_k^{\!\top}}_{(2\times4)(4\times4)(4\times2)}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}\,\bm{\Sigma}\,\bm{J}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\!\top}}_{(2\times6)(6\times6)(6\times2)}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\bm{R}_{\mathrm{cam}}}_{2\times2}.
  \label{eq:Smono}
\end{equation}
The independence assumption behind the vanishing cross terms deserves to be named:
it holds because the navigation solution is produced by a separate subsystem that
does not use the target detections, exactly the separation stated in
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}; were the two coupled, as they would be in a joint
navigation-and-tracking filter, a cross-covariance term would be required.

The middle term of \eqref{eq:Smono} is what this chapter contributes. It inflates
the innovation covariance, and hence the validation gate, by the amount of pixel
uncertainty that the pose error induces \emph{at the current geometry}, so that
the Kalman gain $\bm{K}_k=\bm{P}_{k|k-1}\bm{H}_k^{\top}\bm{S}_k^{-1}$
automatically down-weights measurements taken while the platform pose is poorly
known. The update otherwise follows the standard EKF recursion of
Chapter~\ref{cha:background}. Equation \eqref{eq:Smono} is thus the concrete
instance, for a monocular projective measurement, of the pose-uncertainty
propagation that Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} anticipated once assumption~(i) of its
equivalence proposition is dropped.

\paragraph*{On the unavailability of $\bm{\Sigma}$.} Ideally $\bm{\Sigma}$ would be
read directly from the navigation system at every step, as the covariance that a
Bayesian estimator reports alongside its mean, and the term would then require no
tuning at all. That was not possible here, and the reason is worth stating
precisely rather than leaving as a gap, since it is the single most
important caveat attached to the results of this chapter. The platform's
localization is produced by a LiDAR-inertial odometry solution running on board
the TU~Berlin quadrotor. That solution publishes a pose, but it does
\emph{not} publish an associated covariance: the estimator's uncertainty is
simply not among the quantities exposed on its output interface, and recovering it
would have required instrumenting and re-running the navigation stack itself,
which was outside the scope of the collaboration and, after the return from the
three-month research stay, no longer practicable with the recorded data alone.
Consequently a time-varying $\bm{\Sigma}_k$ could not be used. Rather than discard
the pose uncertainty altogether, which the ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:mono_consistency} shows to be the far worse option, it is modeled
as constant and isotropic, $\bm{\Sigma}=\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}^2\bm{I}_6$, with the
single scale $\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}$ calibrated \emph{a posteriori} from the
consistency analysis. Two consequences follow and should be read into the results.
First, the term captures the \emph{average} pose quality over the flight and cannot
respond to the fact that the navigation error is genuinely larger during the fast
yaw sweeps than in hover, so the filter is somewhat optimistic exactly when it
should be most cautious. Second, isotropy is a modeling convenience rather than a
physical claim: a real LiDAR-inertial solution is markedly more accurate in
attitude than in position, and in yaw than in roll and pitch, so the true
$\bm{\Sigma}$ is far from a multiple of the identity. Replacing the scalar by the
navigation solution's own covariance is a direct extension, requiring only that
the estimator expose it, and is identified as future work in
Chapter~\ref{cha:conclusions}.

\subsection{Temporal Synchronization}
\label{sec:mono_sync}

The camera and the navigation system are asynchronous streams with distinct
clocks and rates, so each image must be paired with the platform pose at its true
\emph{capture} instant. Two effects are corrected. First, a fixed camera latency
$\tau$ (the capture-to-timestamp delay) is subtracted from each frame's timestamp.
Second, the pose is interpolated to the corrected instant: the position by linear
interpolation and the orientation by Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP) of
the unit quaternions, which respects the geometry of $SO(3)$. A pose paired with a
stale or mis-synchronized frame would inject a spurious apparent motion of the
target, so correct synchronization is a prerequisite for the extrinsic-aware
covariance \eqref{eq:Smono} to reflect the true pose uncertainty instead of a
timing artifact.

\subsection{Experimental Platform}
\label{sec:mono_platform}

The data were collected with the UBADRON platform of TU~Berlin, a custom
multirotor developed within the UBASWARM project, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:tub_platform}. It carries a global-shutter camera (a Raspberry Pi
Global Shutter module built on the Sony IMX296 sensor, with a $6$~mm C-mount
lens) operating at $1280\times960$~px. A global shutter is essential on a rotating
platform, because a rolling shutter would skew the target during the aggressive
yaw maneuvers that characterize these flights. The camera is rigidly mounted in an
underslung pod, $0.21$~m below the body origin and pitched $10^\circ$ downward
from the horizontal, and this fixed extrinsic $\bm{T}_{\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{O}}$
is composed with the platform pose to form the world-to-optical transform of
\eqref{eq:mono_h}. The camera intrinsics ($f_x=1592.3$, $f_y=1513.2$,
$c_x=640.5$, $c_y=434.1$~px) and the plumb-bob distortion coefficients were
obtained by an offline checkerboard calibration and held fixed throughout. The
platform pose itself is streamed by the onboard navigation system as a
position--attitude solution, a fused LiDAR-inertial estimate built on an onboard
Livox Avia LiDAR and an inertial measurement unit, which provides the
$(\bm{R}^{wo}_k,\bm{t}^{wo}_k)$ sequence and motivates the covariance
$\bm{\Sigma}$ of \eqref{eq:Smono}.

\paragraph*{Computing environment and what ``real-time'' means here.} The
perception stack is not required to run embedded on the aircraft in the TU~Berlin
search-and-rescue setting: the platform streams its imagery and navigation
solution to a companion ground computer, which is the operating model the team
adopts, and this study follows it. All processing reported in this dissertation,
for this chapter as well as for the simulation studies of
Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:airsim}, was carried out on a single
machine, a Dell laptop with an Intel Core~i9-13900HX processor and an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX~4080 laptop GPU ($12$~GB), using multi-threaded execution to overlap decoding, inference, and
filtering. The recursive filter itself is computationally negligible and runs
comfortably faster than the $20$~Hz frame rate, so the \emph{tracking} stage is
real-time on this hardware. The CNN detector is not: its inference introduces a
latency of roughly two to three seconds behind the live stream, so the composed
detector-plus-tracker chain of this chapter was run offline over the recorded
flight rather than live. This is a throughput limitation of the detector on a
laptop-class GPU, not of the estimator, and it is stated plainly here so that the
accuracy results that follow are not read as a demonstration of onboard real-time
operation. The full three-dimensional pipeline of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim} is
heavier still and further from real time; the corresponding limitation is recorded
in Chapter~\ref{cha:conclusions}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:mono_results}

The methodology above is evaluated on real flight data. This section reports, in
turn, the experimental scenario and how metric ground truth was obtained for a
freely walking person indoors; the localization accuracy of the tracker against
that reference, separating the accuracy of the geometry from the robustness of the
end-to-end chain; and the statistical consistency of the filter, which is where
the pose-uncertainty term of Section~\ref{sec:mono_extrinsic} is put to the test.

\subsection{Experimental Scenario and Ground-Truth Acquisition}
\label{sec:mono_scenario}

The tracker was evaluated on a real flight in an indoor sports hall, in which the
quadrotor hovered and maneuvered over a matted area ($\approx 11.0\times12.0$~m,
whose extent was measured on site) while a single person walked along its
perimeter.\footnote{A video of the real-flight experiment is available at
\url{https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fo/r9j7sqlwngrz60ltsldpr/AOwC3mAlRbmpuSl3Xv06cKo?rlkey=rez4d1ao6k2ccnpku2qlmbpvm&dl=0}.}
The flight lasted
about $150$~s. Importantly for the analysis below, the platform executed full
$360^\circ$ yaw rotations, so the target traversed the entire field of view and
the platform pose varied over its whole range, which is a demanding test of the
pose-uncertainty handling. Although this flight contains a single target, the
estimator is not restricted to one: exactly as in Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}, it
is instantiated once per track behind a SORT-style data-association
stage~\cite{bewley2016sort}, so multi-target operation reduces to running one of
these per-target filters per confirmed track.

Obtaining metric ground truth for a freely walking person indoors, without
instrumenting the subject, is itself non-trivial. We used the room geometry as
the reference. A dense LiDAR point-cloud map of the hall (acquired with a
Livox Mid-360, $\sim 1.8\times10^{6}$ points) provides a centimeter-accurate
metric model of the floor and its landmarks. Reference positions were then
obtained by manual annotation: for a set of $135$ frames spread across the
flight, an operator marked the target's foot point in the image and the
corresponding floor location in a top-down rendering of the LiDAR map, which
yields a paired image/world ground-truth sample per annotated frame. The
annotation uses neither the camera pose nor the detector, so it is independent of
the tracker and provides an unbiased reference. A consistency check confirms its
validity: the annotated trajectory spans $11.9\times12.8$~m, matching the
surveyed mat (here and below, ``surveyed'' means measured on site from the
LiDAR map); the implied walking speed averages $1.04$~m/s with a maximum of
$3.8$~m/s, consistent with a walking person; and the path forms approximately
$1.6$ laps of the mat perimeter, as observed in the video.

\subsection{Localization Accuracy against LiDAR-Referenced Ground Truth}
\label{sec:mono_accuracy}

The tracker produced a continuous ground-plane estimate over the flight, active
in $626$ frames. Comparing it to the manual ground truth at the annotated instants
(matched by timestamp to within $0.5$~s, $128$ paired samples) gives the
ground-plane localization error reported in Figures~\ref{fig:mono_traj}
and~\ref{fig:mono_errors}. The
estimate is the \emph{raw} filter output: no post-hoc alignment, bias removal, or
trajectory registration of any kind is applied, so the numbers reflect the
end-to-end accuracy an onboard consumer would actually receive.

\paragraph*{Terminology.} The error metric used throughout this section is the
Euclidean distance between the estimated and the true target position measured
\emph{in the plane of the floor}, that is, in the $(x,y)$ coordinates of the world
frame $\mathcal{W}$, whose $z$ axis is vertical. It is called the
\emph{ground-plane error} rather than the ``horizontal error'' deliberately: the
latter invites confusion with a plane attached to the \emph{aircraft}, which on a
freely maneuvering quadrotor is tilted with respect to the ground and rotates
continuously during the flight. No component of the error is measured in the
aircraft's own plane. The vertical component is not reported at all, and could not
be: the ground-plane constraint of Section~\ref{sec:mono_groundplane} fixes the
target's height by assumption, so the estimator has no vertical degree of freedom
whose error could be evaluated.

It is worth separating two questions that the single error figure conflates: how
accurate the geometric lift is when the target is correctly detected, and how
robust the end-to-end track is across the whole flight, detector failures
included. We report both. To isolate the geometry, the foot pixel of each
manually annotated frame is lifted through the time-synchronized pose by the
ray--plane intersection \eqref{eq:rayplane} and compared directly to the ground
truth, with no filtering; this per-frame projection attains a median error of
$1.05$~m, an RMSE of $1.36$~m, and a worst case of $3.1$~m. This is the intrinsic
accuracy of the monocular ground-plane method on this flight, and it confirms that
the projective formulation of Section~\ref{sec:mono_method} is sound: with a
correct foot pixel and the recorded pose, the metric position is recovered to
about a meter despite the grazing $\sim 6^\circ$ depression angle at which the
low-altitude platform viewed the target.

This intrinsic accuracy is not uniform with distance, and
Figure~\ref{fig:error_range} makes the dependence explicit. Binning the per-frame
projection error by the target's distance from the camera shows the error rising
from a median of about $0.6$~m for targets at $10$--$12$~m to about $1.7$~m beyond
$18$~m, with a sample correlation of $0.53$ between distance and error and a
trend well described by a quadratic. This is the geometry predicted in
Section~\ref{sec:mono_meas}: a fixed vertical offset of the foot pixel subtends a
ground-range error that grows with distance, and the growth is steepened by the
shallow depression angle, where the viewing ray grazes the floor. It is the
fundamental reason a single camera, lifting a pixel through a ground plane,
degrades with range where a true range sensor would not.

\paragraph*{Two reporting regimes, and why both are given.} The end-to-end error
must be quoted carefully, because two quite different quantities can be computed
from the same $128$ matched samples, and reporting only one of them misleads in
opposite directions. The distinction is between the frames in which the detector
is actually looking at the person and the frames in which it is not. During the
platform's full-circle yaw sweeps the target leaves the field of view entirely;
the detector then fires on background structure, and the resulting false-positive
foot pixel lifts to a position several meters away. Including those frames in a
single aggregate does not measure the localization capability of the system at
all: it measures how badly a wrong detection projects, which is a property of the
detector's precision under loss of view.

The separation between the two regimes is made on purely geometric grounds, with
no tuned threshold and no appeal to the ground truth. A detection is accepted as a
view of the target only if all three of the following hold. First, its
back-projected ray must actually meet the ground plane ahead of the camera: with
$\mathbf{d}=[(u-c_x)/f_x,\;(v-c_y)/f_y,\;1]^{\!\top}$, the ray
$\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{R}_{\mathcal{W}\mathcal{O}}^{\!\top}\mathbf{d}$ and the camera
centre $\mathbf{C}=-\mathbf{R}_{\mathcal{W}\mathcal{O}}^{\!\top}\mathbf{t}$ must
give a positive depth $\lambda=(z_g-C_z)/r_z>0$, which fails outright whenever the
detection lies above the horizon. Second, the resulting floor point must lie no
more than $2$~m outside the surveyed mat. Third, at least one surveyed corner of
the mat must project inside the $1280\times960$ image, so that the camera is
demonstrably still looking at the arena at all. The test uses only the intrinsics,
the recorded pose, and the surveyed room geometry, all of which are available
online; it is the operational criterion the system would use to declare that it
has lost sight of the target, and it is applied here to the error metric exactly
as it would be applied in flight.

Of the $626$ frames in which the detector fired, this test rejects $106$
($16.9\%$): $37$ because the ray never meets the floor, and $69$ because it meets
it far outside the hall. The rejected detections sit high in the image (median row
$v\approx185$, against $v\approx354$ for the accepted ones), that is, at or above
the horizon --- the signature of a detector firing on background structure rather
than on a person standing on the floor.

one that characterizes the estimator.

With the loss-of-view frames excluded, the tracker attains a median ground-plane
error of $1.43$~m and an RMSE of $2.46$~m. The median sits within $0.4$~m of the
$1.05$~m geometric floor established above, which is the substantive result of
this comparison: the recursive filter essentially \emph{preserves} the accuracy of
the underlying single-frame lift rather than degrading it, and what remains is
dominated by the irreducible error of the monocular ground-plane geometry itself.
Including every frame, the median rises modestly to $1.75$~m but the RMSE jumps to
$6.27$~m, with a $90$th percentile of $7.75$~m. That the median barely moves while
the RMSE more than doubles is itself diagnostic: a quadratic penalty is dominated
by a small number of large excursions, so the gap between the two rows of
Table~\ref{tab:mono_accuracy} is not a broad degradation but a few short episodes.

It should be said plainly that the in-view figure is not a clean measure of the
estimator either. The visibility test certifies that the camera was looking at the
mat, not that the detection on it was the person: a residual worst case of $6.9$~m
survives the test, against a worst case of $3.1$~m for the geometric lift on
annotated frames, so a handful of in-view frames still carry a misassociated
detection. The gap between those two worst cases, rather than either median, is the
honest measure of how much of the residual error is attributable to the detector
rather than to the geometry.

These figures are best read against the scale and difficulty of the task: a single
uninstrumented camera, recovering metric position through a ground-plane lift, on
a platform undergoing full rotations, with a navigation pose that is itself
estimated. A median of about $1.4$~m over a $\sim 12$~m working area, with the
target $10$--$20$~m from the camera and the line of sight grazing the floor at
about $6^\circ$, is an accurate localization from monocular cues alone, and it
improves on consumer GNSS, which this pipeline does not use.

\paragraph*{Reading the trajectory plot.} Figure~\ref{fig:mono_traj} shows the
estimated track against the ground truth in the plane of the floor, and it repays
a careful reading, since three different objects are superimposed on it. The
\emph{green} markers are the manually annotated ground-truth positions, obtained
from the LiDAR map and independent of both the camera pose and the detector. The
continuous track shaded from yellow to red is the raw EKF estimate, and the
\emph{color encodes flight time}, from yellow at the start of the flight to red at
the end, on the scale given by the color bar on the right of the figure; it is not
a second variable and not an error magnitude. The \emph{gray} points are the estimates
rejected by the geometric visibility test, plotted only so that their location can
be seen; they are not part of the track, and most of them lie far enough outside
the mat to fall outside the plotted window entirely. The dashed rectangle is
the surveyed perimeter of the matted area.

Read this way, the plot shows that the estimate reproduces the perimeter walk and
overlays the ground truth for most of the flight, with a handful of straight
strays into the interior of the mat. The strays are not a drift of the filter but
its response to the loss-of-view episodes: the estimate is pulled toward a
spurious detection and then returns, and because the intervening frames are
propagated by a constant-velocity model the excursion is drawn as a straight
segment. The gray points cluster in exactly those intervals.

\paragraph*{Systematic effects and where the error comes from.} Three distinct
mechanisms contribute to the error, and separating them is what the analysis above
is for.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Range.} The dominant systematic effect on the correctly detected
  frames. A fixed vertical offset of the foot pixel subtends a ground-range error
  that grows with distance, and the growth is amplified by the $\sim 6^\circ$
  depression angle at which this low-altitude platform views the floor, where the
  viewing ray is nearly parallel to the plane it is being intersected with. The
  measured dependence, from a median of about $0.6$~m at $10$--$12$~m to about
  $1.7$~m beyond $18$~m with a correlation of $0.53$, is reported in
  Figure~\ref{fig:error_range}. Because the mat is walked around its perimeter
  while the platform hovers near one side, range and error co-vary through the
  flight, which is why the error series shows a slow modulation on top of its
  baseline.
  \item \textbf{Platform rotation.} The yaw sweeps act through two separate
  channels, and it is worth not conflating them. The first is \emph{geometric and
  benign}: the target crosses the field of view and eventually leaves it, so the
  measurement stream is interrupted and the track must coast or re-initialize.
  The second is \emph{estimation-theoretic}: the navigation pose is least accurate
  precisely during aggressive rotation, and an attitude error maps into a pixel
  displacement through the rotational block of the extrinsic Jacobian
  \eqref{eq:Jext}, whose magnitude \emph{grows} with target distance. This second
  channel does not show up prominently in the point error, because the
  re-initialization logic re-anchors the state to a fresh measurement, but it is
  exactly what the consistency analysis of
  Section~\ref{sec:mono_consistency} exposes, and it is the reason the pose
  covariance must be propagated.
  \item \textbf{Detector precision under loss of view.} The cause of the two large
  excursions, and of the entire RMSE tail. It is a failure of the
  \emph{detector}, not of the geometry or of the filter: the per-frame geometric
  projection on correctly detected frames never exceeds $3.1$~m, and the filter's
  re-initialization (Section~\ref{sec:mono_groundplane}) recovers the track as
  soon as the true target is re-detected. The remedy belongs at the detection and
  track-management level, through more selective detection and explicit
  loss-of-view handling, and not in the projection.
\end{enumerate}

One further observation deserves recording, since it was raised during the
examination. An earlier version of these results reported a median error of
$4.3$~m and a $90$th percentile of $10.3$~m, roughly three times what is reported
here. Those figures were not a property of the geometry. An audit of the
projection against the independent floor-plan ground truth, carried out
separately from the filter, established that the single-frame lift was correct all
along (median $1.05$~m); what produced the larger numbers was the constant-velocity
model being propagated open-loop across the long detection gaps left by the yaw
sweeps, so that any nonzero velocity estimate was extrapolated for the whole gap.
Bounding that open-loop horizon and re-initializing the track by ray--plane
intersection after a gap, as described in Section~\ref{sec:mono_groundplane}, is
what brought the error down to the values reported here. The episode is recorded
because it illustrates a general point about this class of estimator: over a long
measurement outage, a constant-velocity model is not a conservative choice but an
actively dangerous one, and the safe behavior is to discard the stale velocity
rather than to trust it.

A second point is recorded for the same reason. Read side by side, the error
series of Figure~\ref{fig:mono_errors} and the map of Figure~\ref{fig:mono_traj}
appear at first to disagree: the series reaches ${\approx}22$~m, whereas no point
drawn inside the map lies that far from any ground-truth sample. The two were
checked against each other sample by sample, and they agree exactly; the
appearance of a contradiction is an artifact of the map's axes. During the
loss-of-view intervals the filter is dragged to positions tens of meters outside
the hall --- the extreme case is $(40.4,\,17.4)$~m, against a ground truth at
$(19.5,\,10.9)$~m --- and those estimates simply fall outside the plotted window.
Of the $106$ rejected estimates, $85$ lie outside it. This is precisely why the
out-of-view frames are now identified by an explicit geometric test and reported
as a separate regime rather than folded into a single aggregate: an error
statistic whose tail is invisible in the corresponding figure is an invitation to
misread both.

The role of the platform rotation, and of the pose uncertainty it induces, is
examined next through the filter's consistency rather than its point error.

\subsection{Filter Consistency and the Role of Pose-Uncertainty Propagation}
\label{sec:mono_consistency}

The accuracy figures above are an end-to-end summary. The more incisive question
for an estimator feeding a collision-avoidance layer is whether the filter is
\emph{consistent}, that is, whether its reported covariance matches its actual
error
(Chapter~\ref{cha:background}). With no per-step ground truth for the full state,
consistency is assessed through the Normalized Innovation Squared (NIS),
$\eta_k=\bm{\nu}_k^{\top}\bm{S}_k^{-1}\bm{\nu}_k$, which for the two-dimensional
pixel innovation follows a $\chi^2_2$ law with mean $2$ under a consistent filter.
The NIS bears directly on the contribution of
Section~\ref{sec:mono_extrinsic}, because the pose-uncertainty term in
\eqref{eq:Smono} acts entirely through $\bm{S}_k$.

To isolate that term we performed an ablation over the pose-uncertainty scale
$\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}$ (with $\bm{\Sigma}=\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}\bm{I}_6$),
re-running the filter on the recorded detections and poses; Figure~\ref{fig:mono_nis}
reports the result. With the extrinsic term switched off
($\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{0}$), which treats the platform pose as exact, the mean NIS is
$3.4\times10^{4}$, four orders of magnitude above the consistent value of $2$.
The filter is severely \emph{overconfident}: it reports a tiny innovation
covariance while the actual innovations, driven by the unmodeled pose error, are
large. As $\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}$ increases, the mean NIS falls monotonically and
crosses the consistent value $\bar\eta=2$ near
$\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}\approx10^{-3}$; beyond that the filter becomes
conservative (underconfident). The time series
(Figure~\ref{fig:mono_nis}b) confirms that the pose-off filter sits far above the
$95\%$ $\chi^2_2$ gate throughout the flight, while the calibrated filter stays
near the consistent regime.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap6/nis_consistency.pdf}
  \caption{Filter consistency and the role of pose-uncertainty propagation.
  (a) Mean NIS as a function of the pose-uncertainty scale
  $\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}$. Without the extrinsic term ($\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{0}$,
  ``OFF'') the filter is overconfident by four orders of magnitude; propagating
  the pose covariance brings the filter to statistical consistency
  ($\bar\eta=2$, green) near $\sigma_{\mathrm{pose}}\approx10^{-3}$. (b) NIS over
  the flight for the pose-off and calibrated filters; the consistent band and the
  $95\%$ $\chi^2_2$ gate are shown for reference.}
  \label{fig:mono_nis}
\end{figure}

The ablation makes the methodological point concrete. The extrinsic term of
\eqref{eq:Smono} is not a cosmetic refinement. Omitting it renders the filter
statistically meaningless, with a covariance that understates the true error by
four orders of magnitude, whereas including it, with a single scalar tuned to the
navigation solution's quality, restores consistency. The downstream avoidance
logic depends on this kind of consistent covariance, as emphasized in the
introduction, so propagating the pose uncertainty is what makes the monocular
estimate trustworthy as well as accurate.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:mono_discussion}

This chapter set out to test the generality of the
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} framework, and the result is affirmative with one
caveat. The same constant-velocity EKF, with the spherical measurement replaced
by a ground-constrained pinhole projection, tracks a walking person from a real,
freely maneuvering UAV using a single camera and an estimated platform pose. The
typical end-to-end accuracy, a median ground-plane error of $1.43$~m with the
target in view, rising to $1.75$~m when the loss-of-view episodes are
included, over a $\sim 12$~m arena from monocular cues and without GNSS, against a
geometric floor of $1.05$~m for the unfiltered single-frame lift, shows that the
estimator survives the transition from simulation to the real world and recovers
metric position to about a meter even at the grazing $\sim 6^\circ$ depression
angle of this low-altitude flight.

It is worth being blunt about how good these numbers are and are not. A median of
roughly $1.4$~m is a respectable result for the sensing available, but it is an
order of magnitude coarser than the ${\approx}0.2$~m the range-sensing tracker of
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} achieves in simulation, and the error distribution has
a heavy tail that a collision-avoidance function could not simply ignore: the
$90$th percentile over all frames is $7.75$~m. Nor is the accuracy uniform, since
it degrades roughly quadratically with target range and would degrade further at
the larger standoffs a real sense-and-avoid engagement involves. The honest
summary is that monocular ground-plane tracking is adequate for the
search-and-rescue task it was built for, where the objective is to \emph{find} a
person on the terrain to within a couple of meters, and is not by itself adequate
for close-in collision avoidance, where the tail of the error distribution matters
more than its median. That is an argument for the camera--point-cloud fusion of
the full pipeline rather than against the estimator, whose behavior under these
conditions is exactly what the consistency analysis showed it to be: correct about
how uncertain it is, once the pose covariance is propagated.

The caveat is the platform pose, and it ties this chapter back to the theory of
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}. There, the global and local EKF formulations were
proven equivalent under three assumptions, the first of which was a \emph{known}
platform pose; that chapter flagged the uncertain-pose case as the regime where
the equivalence breaks and a pose-covariance term must enter the innovation. This
chapter is the empirical realization of that regime. The extrinsic-aware
innovation covariance \eqref{eq:Smono} is the concrete form of that term for a
projective measurement, and the NIS ablation (Figure~\ref{fig:mono_nis}) shows
that it is necessary: without it the filter is overconfident by four orders of
magnitude. In short, propagating the covariance of the pose estimate into the
filter is what makes the estimate consistent, and the framework generalizes to
this real-world setting provided that term is included.

The error analysis also delineates the limits of monocular ground-plane tracking
and points to the remedies. The largest errors are not a failure of the geometry
or the filter but of the \emph{detector under loss of view}: during the platform's
full-circle yaw sweeps the target leaves the field of view, the person detector
momentarily fires on background structure, and the false-positive foot pixel lifts
to a position several meters off. The remedy is at the detection and
track-management level, more selective detection and explicit track-coasting or
re-acquisition logic during loss of view, rather than in the projection. A second
limitation is structural and sets the accuracy floor even when detection is
correct: the monocular lift trades the unobservable depth for the known target
height, so any error in the foot-point pixel that stands in for the ground contact
maps to a planar error that grows with range, and this sensitivity is amplified at
the shallow depression angle of a low-altitude platform, where the viewing ray
grazes the floor. This is the fundamental reason a single camera cannot match a
true range sensor, and it motivates the camera--point-cloud fusion that the full
pipeline (Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}) restores through the 3D detection front-end
of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}, where the depth need not be assumed because a point
cloud is available.

Read alongside Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}, the two tracking studies bracket the
same estimator from opposite ends, a controlled range-sensing known-pose
simulation and an uncertain-pose bearing-only real-world deployment, and together
they substantiate the dissertation's claim that one recursive estimator, properly
instrumented with its uncertainty model, serves the tracking stage across
perception modalities.

With the tracking stage now established in both regimes, what remains unbuilt is
the front-end that feeds it. Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:monocular}
have taken their measurements as given: a synthetic range--bearing reading in the
first case, and a person-detector bounding box in the second. The next chapter
constructs the measurement itself for the full pipeline of
Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}. It builds a purpose-made synthetic aerial dataset in
the AirSim simulator to overcome the scarcity of annotated three-dimensional
aerial data identified in Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}, trains the
two-dimensional detector and the frustum-based point-cloud network on it,
identifies foreground extraction as the factor that governs three-dimensional
localization accuracy, and compares the adopted two-stage frustum design against
the single-stage voxel alternative on a moving-observer campaign. Its output, a
range, azimuth, elevation, and size with a covariance, is exactly the measurement
that the filter of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} consumes, which is where the
detection front-end and the tracker of this dissertation meet.
