This chapter develops the detection front-end of the perception pipeline of
Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}: the two stages that turn raw camera and point-cloud
data into the metric, per-object measurements that the tracker of
Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:monocular} consumes. Because annotated
three-dimensional aerial data are scarce (Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}), the
chapter first constructs a fully-annotated synthetic dataset in the AirSim
simulator \cite{shah2018airsim}, then trains and evaluates the
two-dimensional detector, the frustum-based three-dimensional detector, and a set
of principled alternatives, on a held-out campaign of $60$ video sequences with a
moving observer. The treatment is self-contained but reuses the coordinate-frame,
network, and metric conventions fixed in Chapter~\ref{cha:background}.

\section{Overview and Objectives}

The pipeline of Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline} lifts each two-dimensional detection
into a viewing frustum and regresses an amodal three-dimensional box from the
point cloud cropped by that frustum, following the Frustum~PointNets lineage
\cite{qi2018frustum,qi2017pointnetpp}. Training this front-end for the aerial,
urban sense-and-avoid setting raises three obstacles that this chapter
addresses in turn: (i) there is no large-scale annotated aerial 3D dataset
(as established in Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}, the only such benchmark is one
year old and itself synthetic \cite{ye2024uav3d}); (ii) the simulator's own
annotation interfaces are unreliable for the small drone meshes used here, which
forces a synthetic-composition strategy for the 2D detector and a manual
geometric construction for the 3D boxes; and (iii) the natural point-cloud
normalization scheme degrades 3D localization when the background dominates the
frustum, a failure that motivates a dedicated foreground-extraction step. The
objectives are therefore to (a)~build a configurable, fully-annotated aerial
dataset; (b)~design and train the 2D$\rightarrow$frustum$\rightarrow$3D detector;
(c)~quantify its detection and localization accuracy with the
multi-object-tracking metrics standard in the driving literature; and (d)~compare
the adopted two-stage frustum design against the single-stage voxel paradigm that
the related work (Section~\ref{cha:relatedwork}) leaves untested on UAVs.

\section{The AirSim Synthetic Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Simulation Environment and Scenarios}

Data were generated with the AirSim plugin for Unreal Engine
\cite{shah2018airsim}, driven over its remote-procedure-call (RPC) interface from
a Python client. Three urban environments span the operational envelope of Urban
Air Mobility: a low-rise residential neighborhood (\textsc{AirSimNH}), a dense
high-rise downtown (\textsc{City}), and a vegetated coastal scene
(\textsc{Coastline}). Each carries an ego platform equipped with a
forward-looking RGB camera, a planar-depth camera, and a $64$-beam LiDAR, all
rigidly co-located ($0.35$~m forward, $0.5$~m below the body origin) and pitched
$-15^\circ$ so that their fields of view overlap and share a single optical frame
$\mathcal{O}$ (Chapter~\ref{cha:background}). The camera is modeled as an ideal
pinhole at $1280\times720$ with a $90^\circ$ horizontal field of view, giving
focal lengths $f_x=f_y=640$~px and principal point $(c_x,c_y)=(640,360)$~px;
the simulated camera introduces no lens distortion, so the pinhole projection of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pinhole} is exact and no undistortion step is needed (unlike the
real camera of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}). Empirical calibration against the
simulator's relative-pose readout confirmed square pixels, correcting an initial
$f_y$ estimate based on the vertical field of view. AirSim operates internally in a North--East--Down world convention; all point
clouds and detections in this chapter are expressed in the optical frame
$\mathcal{O}$ and lifted to the world frame with the platform pose using the
$Z$--$Y$--$X$ rotation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:euler}.

The depth camera, rather than the LiDAR, is the primary source of the working
point cloud. At UAM altitudes the targets are seen against the sky, where the
LiDAR returns nothing; even after widening its vertical field of view the beams
that strike a distant drone are too few to localize it, whereas the dense planar
depth image yields $10^5$--$3\times10^5$ points per frame and resolves the target
reliably. The LiDAR is retained as an auxiliary, frame-aligned channel. A point
in the optical frame is recovered from a depth pixel $(u,v)$ with depth $z$ by the
inverse pinhole map $\bm{p}^{\mathcal{O}}=\big[(u-c_x)z/f_x,\,(v-c_y)z/f_y,\,z\big]^\top$,
retaining returns with $0.1\le z<250$~m.

To make the simulator's image and its reported poses mutually consistent, the
scene is frozen with \texttt{simPause} during the per-frame acquisition of image,
depth, detections, and poses; without it, the targets drift by about one meter in
the half-second spanned by the successive RPC queries, misaligning the rendered
object from its annotation. Targets and ego are re-teleported to their planned
poses at the start of every frame, since the simulator's hovering controller does
not hold position to the required tolerance.

\subsection{Visual-Mesh Augmentation and Geometric 3D Annotation}
\label{sec:visquad}

The native multirotor mesh is too small (sub-meter envelope) to be detected
reliably by a 2D detector or sampled densely by the depth camera at the
$20$--$100$~m ranges of interest. Each target therefore carries a rigidly
attached visual quadrotor mesh, enlarged by a factor of four relative to the
native mesh (denoted \emph{VisQuad}) so that it is both visible to the detector
and struck by enough depth pixels at those ranges, and re-pose-synchronized to
its carrier at $30$~Hz; the 2D detector is trained on this mesh and the same mesh
is assumed at inference. Its axis-aligned full extents, measured in-simulator, are
$3.01\times3.93\times2.79$~m; multiplying each by a $1.38$ margin that accounts
for the spinning rotors omitted from the simulator's bounding box, and halving,
gives the half-extents $\bm{e}=[2.08,\,2.71,\,1.93]^\top$~m used for annotation.

Importantly, the ground-truth 3D box is \emph{not} read from the simulator's
detection API, whose relative-pose output carries a systematic $\approx 5$~m pitch
offset in the vertical axis and whose reported box is undersized. Instead it is
constructed geometrically from the true global pose. The eight corners of the
target's axis-aligned box, rotated by the target yaw, are transformed into the
optical frame by the inverse platform rotation, the camera lever arm, and the
$-15^\circ$ pitch, and the amodal box is read off the transformed corners; a
corner behind the image plane ($z<0.5$~m) rejects the sample. This manual
construction is what makes the 3D supervision trustworthy and is reused to score
localization at test time.

\subsection{The Automatic-Labeling Problem and the Synthetic Solution}
\label{sec:labeling}

Training the 2D detector requires bounding-box labels at scale, and three
automatic routes through the simulator were attempted and abandoned, each for a
concrete reason. \emph{(i)~Semantic segmentation.} AirSim maps object identifiers
to colors, but in the version used the drone blueprints were not assigned unique
stencil identifiers, so every target shared a color with the background or with
each other; recovering boxes from the masks would have required recompiling the
Unreal environments to set the identifiers, which the pre-built binaries did not
permit. A fallback heuristic (small dark blobs) produced too many false positives
to be usable. \emph{(ii)~The detection API.} \texttt{simGetDetections} returned no
matches for any of more than twenty mesh-name filter patterns, and even where it
responded it missed roughly a third of the targets that were geometrically in
view. \emph{(iii)~Projection of the known pose.} Projecting the true target pose
through the pinhole model gave boxes with $5$--$30$~px systematic error from a
frame-convention mismatch and offered no independent ground truth against which to
validate them.

The adopted solution decouples labeling from the simulator entirely by
\emph{synthetic composition}, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}. A library
of $196$ drone \emph{sprites} (cropped 2D image patches of the drone, in the
sense used in computer graphics) is captured once, spanning $12$ yaw $\times$ $3$
pitch $\times$ $3$ roll orientations at five ranges; each sprite is segmented from
the sky by a brightness threshold and stored as an RGBA patch with its capture range.
The VisQuad mesh is near-black against a bright sky, so a single grayscale cut
separates it cleanly, whereas an RGB difference (which requires two synchronized
captures) or segmentation (route~(i)) would not. Drone-free backgrounds are
then captured across the three environments, and training frames are produced by
alpha-compositing one to five sprites onto a background at random positions and
scales ($0.4$--$1.5\times$), with at most $50\%$ mutual occlusion and small
sprites confined to the upper image where distant drones appear. Because the
sprite is pasted at a known location, the 2D box is \emph{pixel-exact}, and the
target range follows in closed form from the scale ratio,
$\rho = \rho_0\,(w_0/w)$, where $\rho_0,w_0$ are the capture range and width and
$w$ the composited width. The procedure yields roughly $2{,}500$ fully-labeled
frames at negligible cost and is immune to the simulator's annotation defects.

\subsection{Sensor Channels, Annotations, and Evaluation Campaign}
\label{sec:campaign}

The point-cloud detector is trained on the depth-derived clouds of the collected
environments (three scenes, $1{,}000$ frames each, RGB $+$ depth $+$ LiDAR $+$
segmentation $+$ ego pose, with the geometric 3D boxes of
Section~\ref{sec:visquad}). Evaluation uses a separate, more demanding
\emph{campaign} of $60$ recorded video sequences ($20$ per environment,
$75$--$110$ frames at ${\approx}5$~Hz) designed to stress the full pipeline:
each sequence randomizes the target range regime (near $18$--$32$~m, mid
$32$--$50$~m, far $50$--$78$~m), the time of day ($07$h--$18$h), the weather
(clear, light fog, light rain), and the number of targets ($2$--$3$). The
\textbf{observer also moves}, oscillating in three dimensions and in
yaw, so that the evaluation exercises tracking from a moving platform exactly as
in Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}. Per-frame ground truth is the true global pose of
every target. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the data.

\section{Detection Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Two-Dimensional Detection}

The first stage is a YOLOv11-small detector
\cite{redmon2016yolo,ultralytics2024yolo11} ($\approx 10$~M parameters), a
one-stage network that regresses boxes and class scores in a single pass and
meets the real-time budget of an onboard system. It is trained for the single
class \emph{drone} on the synthetic frames of Section~\ref{sec:labeling} at
$1280$-pixel input resolution with standard mosaic, HSV, and scale augmentation,
for $80$ epochs using the default Ultralytics training recipe (SGD with Nesterov
momentum, weight decay, and a cosine learning-rate schedule). Its output for each
frame is a set of boxes $\{b_i\}$ with confidences $\{s^{\mathrm{2D}}_i\}$ that
seed the frustum stage; on an RTX-class GPU the detector runs at roughly
$5$~ms per frame.

\subsection{Frustum Proposal and the Normalization Problem}
\label{sec:frustum}

Each 2D box $b_i=(u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2)$, enlarged by $25\%$, defines a viewing frustum;
the points whose pinhole projection $\big(u,v\big)=\big(f_x x^{\mathcal{O}}/z+c_x,\,
f_y y^{\mathcal{O}}/z+c_y\big)$ falls inside it are gathered and resampled to a
fixed cardinality of $N=4096$. The point set is centered on its centroid and
scaled to a unit ball before being fed to the network, the standard PointNet
normalization. This is where a subtle but decisive failure arises. When the
frustum contains background surface behind the target (foliage, a fa\c{c}ade, or
the ground), those far points dominate the scale, compressing the drone into a small
fraction of the normalized volume; the classifier becomes under-confident and, more
importantly, the regressed centroid is pulled toward the background, inflating the
3D localization error to roughly $3$~m. Figure~\ref{fig:depthband} shows the
effect on a real frustum: the target occupies a tight cluster near $30$~m while a
long background tail at $60$--$72$~m sets the normalization scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap7/frustum_depthband.pdf}
  \caption{The frustum-normalization problem on real data. (a)~Top view of a
  frustum point cloud: the drone (the four-rotor cluster near $30$~m) and a
  background tail at $60$--$72$~m. (b)~Range histogram of the same frustum. The
  background tail sets the maximum-distance normalization scale and compresses the
  target; the depth band (shaded) retains only the foreground, restoring metric
  resolution.}
  \label{fig:depthband}
\end{figure}

Two remedies are studied. A robust scale (the $95$th percentile of the point
ranges rather than the maximum) was found to be insufficient: clipping the scale
does not remove the background points, so the regressed centroid remains biased
(Section~\ref{sec:results-norm}). The effective remedy is to \emph{remove} the
background: a \emph{depth band} anchors on the nearest surface
($z_0$, the $5$th percentile of frustum range) and discards every point beyond
$z_0+\Delta$, where $\Delta$ is a fixed band depth (a few meters, set to comfortably
contain a drone) that defines how far behind the nearest surface points are kept;
this isolates the target before normalization (Figure~\ref{fig:depthband_diag}).
This heuristic is simple
and effective but brittle to a frontal occluder; it is therefore replaced, below,
by a learned foreground segmentation.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{Cap7/depthband_diagram.pdf}
  \caption{The depth band, viewed from above. The 2D box defines a viewing
  frustum; the foreground drone forms a tight cluster near the nearest surface
  $z_0$, while a long background tail (a fa\c{c}ade or foliage) extends behind it.
  Keeping only the points within $[z_0,\,z_0+\Delta]$ removes the background that
  would otherwise dominate the point-cloud normalization scale.}
  \label{fig:depthband_diag}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Detection with PointNet}
\label{sec:pointnet}

The 3D detector is a PointNet\texttt{++} \cite{qi2017pointnetpp} with three
set-abstraction levels (sampling $512$ then $128$ centroids with ball-query radii
$0.2$ and $0.4$~m and per-level multilayer perceptrons of widths
$[64,64,128]$, $[128,128,256]$, and $[256,512,1024]$), followed by a shared
fully-connected head that branches into three outputs: an objectness logit
(drone vs. false positive), the box center offset $\in\mathbb{R}^3$, and the box
size $\in\mathbb{R}^3$. Figure~\ref{fig:pn_topology} shows this topology.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap7/pointnet_topology.pdf}
  \caption{Topology of the frustum 3D detector: a PointNet\texttt{++} with three
  hierarchical set-abstraction (SA) levels that progressively subsample the
  $N{=}4096$ frustum points and aggregate local geometry, a shared fully-connected
  trunk, and three heads (objectness, box center, box size). The learned
  foreground segmentation (Section~\ref{sec:segnet}) masks the SA features before
  the box head.}
  \label{fig:pn_topology}
\end{figure} The objectness branch is trained with the focal loss
\cite{lin2017focal} to counter the foreground/background imbalance, and the center
and size branches with the smooth-$L_1$ loss restricted to positive samples,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{focal}}(\hat{s},\,s^{\star})
              + \lambda_c\,\mathbf{1}[s^{\star}{=}1]\,\mathrm{smooth}_{L_1}(\hat{\bm{c}},\bm{c}^{\star})
              + \lambda_s\,\mathbf{1}[s^{\star}{=}1]\,\mathrm{smooth}_{L_1}(\hat{\bm{d}},\bm{d}^{\star}),
  \label{eq:pn_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function (equal to $1$ when its
argument holds and $0$ otherwise), so the regression terms act only on positive
samples ($s^{\star}{=}1$), with $\lambda_c=5$, $\lambda_s=1$. The network is optimized with Adam
($10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) under a cosine schedule, with a
class-balanced sampler and yaw-rotation/jitter augmentation. The detector
estimates one object per frustum; its center, lifted to the world frame, is the
position measurement, and its range, azimuth, and elevation
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:spherical}) are precisely the spherical measurement
$\bm{z}=[\rho,\alpha,\beta,r]^\top$ that the EKF of
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} ingests. This is the formal point of contact between
the detector of this chapter and the tracker.

A whole-cloud variant in the spirit of PointPainting \cite{vora2020pointpainting},
which paints every point with the 2D confidence rather than cropping, was also
trained but reached a lower validation $F_1$ ($0.50$ vs. $0.73$ for the frustum
crop) and is not pursued.

\subsection{Learned Foreground Extraction and an Efficient Alternative}
\label{sec:segnet}

To replace the depth-band heuristic with a principled component, a
segmentation head is added that classifies each frustum point as
target or background, in the manner of the original Frustum~PointNets instance
segmentation \cite{qi2018frustum}. Per-point features (a $64$-dimensional local
embedding concatenated with a $1024$-dimensional global descriptor) feed a
point-wise classifier supervised by the in-box membership of each point; the box
head then pools only the foreground-weighted features. This \emph{learns} which
points belong to the drone; unlike the depth band, it is robust to a foreground
occluder, and it attains the best localization of any frustum
variant. A second alternative, a Frustum~ConvNet \cite{wang2019fconvnet} that
slices the frustum along depth into $16$ bins and applies a one-dimensional
convolution across slices, was also implemented; it matches the depth band
without any explicit cropping.

\subsection{A Single-Stage Voxel Alternative}
\label{sec:voxel}

The two-stage frustum design inherits the recall ceiling of its 2D detector: a
target the YOLO stage misses can never be localized. To quantify the cost of this
dependence we also implement the single-stage paradigm that the related work
(Section~\ref{cha:relatedwork}) cites but never evaluates on UAVs. A
PointPillars encoder \cite{lang2019pointpillars} voxelizes the \emph{whole} scene
into a bird's-eye-view pillar grid ($1.25$~m cells over a
$80\times88$~m region) and a two-dimensional convolutional backbone with a
center-based detection head \cite{zhou2019objects} predicts a target heatmap and
box regression, detecting directly in 3D without a 2D proposal. Three variants
probe what drives its accuracy: hand-crafted pillar features
(occupancy, mean height, maximum return); \emph{learned} pillar features (a
per-pillar mini-PointNet, the PFN of \cite{lang2019pointpillars}); and a dense 3D
voxel backbone with $3$D convolutions in the style of SECOND
\cite{yan2018second}. Two negative controls are also reported: a finer
($0.7$~m) grid and a three-frame temporal accumulation with ego-motion
compensation.

\subsection{Tracking Stage: 2D Association and 3D Filtering}
\label{sec:tracker}

The pipeline closes with a tracking stage built from two parts, and every tracking
metric reported in this chapter is produced by it (Figure~\ref{fig:tracker}a).
The two parts separate \emph{which detection belongs to which track} (a 2D problem,
solved on the reliable image boxes) from \emph{where the track is in 3D} (estimated
by the recursive filter of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}).

\paragraph*{Identity association (2D).} The association follows the ideas of the
SORT tracker \cite{bewley2016sort}: a per-track motion prediction, an
intersection-over-union cost, and a Hungarian assignment. At frame $k$ the
detector returns a set of boxes with fused confidences. Non-maximum suppression
(IoU threshold $0.4$) first removes duplicates, so each object yields a single box
(the deduplication of Figure~\ref{fig:qual}). Each surviving box $\bm{b}_i$ is then assigned to an active
track $j$ by minimizing the cost
\begin{equation}
  C_{ij} \;=\; 1 - \mathrm{IoU}\!\big(\bm{b}_i,\,\hat{\bm{b}}_j\big),
  \qquad
  \hat{\bm{b}}_j = \bm{b}_j^{(k-1)} + \bm{v}_j,
  \label{eq:assoc_cost}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bm{b}}_j$ is track $j$'s box propagated by its (exponentially smoothed)
box velocity $\bm{v}_j$; the optimal assignment is obtained with the Hungarian
algorithm and a pair is accepted only if $\mathrm{IoU}\ge\tau_{\mathrm{IoU}}=0.3$.
An unmatched detection spawns a tentative track; a track is \emph{confirmed} once it
reaches $H_{\min}=3$ hits, is \emph{coasted} --- propagated by prediction with no
measurement --- through up to $C_{\max}=4$ consecutive misses, and is deleted after
$A_{\max}=8$ frames without a hit. Performing the association on the 2D boxes rather
than on the noisier 3D positions is what keeps identities stable. This multi-object
association and lifecycle are precisely the part of the tracker that the
single-target filter of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} does not address.

\paragraph*{Measurement construction.} The association leaves each confirmed track
with at most one detection per frame; the bridge to the filter converts that
detection into the spherical measurement the filter expects. With the detection's
estimated global position $\hat{\bm{p}}_k$ and the known platform pose
$(\bm{R}_k,\bm{p}^p_k)$, the relative position in the sensor frame is
$\bm{p}^c_k=\bm{R}_k^{\top}(\hat{\bm{p}}_k-\bm{p}^p_k)$ as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rel_local}, and the measurement is its spherical map
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:spherical}) augmented by the size estimate,
$\bm{z}_k=[\rho_k,\alpha_k,\beta_k,r_k]^{\top}$. The range $\rho_k=\lVert\bm{p}^c_k\rVert$
therefore carries the distance-dependent noise $\sigma_\rho=0.05\,\rho_k$ (floored at
$0.5$~m) of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}, so that distant detections are trusted less.

\paragraph*{State estimation (3D).} Each confirmed track instantiates one Extended
Kalman Filter \emph{of the exact form derived in Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}} --- the
seven-state object model $[x,y,z,\dot x,\dot y,\dot z,r]$, the constant-velocity
transition with white-acceleration process noise, and the spherical measurement
model with its Jacobian --- reused here without modification, one filter per track.
At every frame the filter predicts; on a frame with an associated detection it
updates with $\bm{z}_k$, and during coasting it predicts only, which is what
supplies a 3D position through the detection gaps. The track's reported
three-dimensional position --- and hence the localization error of
Section~\ref{sec:results} --- is the \emph{filtered} estimate $\hat{\bm{p}}_{k|k}$,
together with the covariance $\bm{P}_{k|k}$ that quantifies its uncertainty for the
avoidance layer. The detection front-end of this chapter thus feeds the same
range--bearing filter developed and validated in Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}; the
monocular tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} is a variant of the same
framework, adapted to a bearing-only measurement and a different state, rather
than this identical filter. Together these realize the end-to-end pipeline of
Chapter~\ref{cha:pipeline}. Table~\ref{tab:tracker} lists the parameters of both
parts.

Because association uses the 2D boxes and the AMOTA metric sweeps the detection

the filter \cite{weng2020ab3dmot}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Metrics}

Three families of metrics are reported, following the 3D multi-object-tracking
practice of \cite{weng2020ab3dmot}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Detection:} precision, recall, and $F_1$ at a fixed operating
  point.
  \item \textbf{Tracking:} the average multi-object tracking accuracy and
  precision (AMOTA, AMOTP), which integrate the CLEAR-MOT accuracy over the full
  recall range and are therefore largely detector-threshold independent; the
  single-operating-point MOTA at a $0.5$ association threshold; the identity score
  IDF1; and the number of identity switches.
  \item \textbf{Localization:} the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and mean
  absolute error of the 3D centroid against ground truth, stratified by range,
  with ground-truth/track association by 3D distance under a gate of a few meters.
\end{itemize}
AMOTA is the headline tracking number because, by sweeping the detection
threshold, it measures the quality of the detector--tracker chain rather than a
single tuned point.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Protocol}

The 2D detector and the frustum 3D networks are trained on the synthetic and
collected data of Section~\ref{sec:dataset} and evaluated on the entire $60$-sequence
campaign, which is disjoint from their training data and thus a fully held-out test.
The single-stage voxel networks, lacking a separate large training corpus, are
trained on the campaign itself under an $80/20$ split by sequence; to verify that
this does not inflate their numbers, all voxel results were recomputed on the
$12$ held-out sequences alone and agree with the full-campaign figures to within
$0.02$ AMOTA and $0.02$~m RMSE (e.g., the best voxel model scores AMOTA $0.67$ and
RMSE $0.85$~m held-out, against $0.68$ and $0.87$~m on the full set), confirming
that the comparison below is not an artifact of in-sample evaluation. Inference
runs offline: one heavy pass per sequence dumps detections, from which all metrics
are recomputed cheaply while sweeping the detection threshold.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Two-Dimensional Detection}

Trained on the pixel-exact synthetic frames, the YOLOv11-small detector reaches
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50}=0.994$ on a held-out synthetic split
(Figure~\ref{fig:yolo}), confirming that synthetic composition supplies clean,
abundant supervision for the 2D stage. For reference, an earlier detector trained
on the simulator's own (sparse, API-derived) labels of the collected set reached
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50}=0.73$ with $0.64$ recall. This recall deficit motivated
the synthetic route and, as shown next, caps the recall of the two-stage
pipeline.

  synthetic composites attains $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}=0.994$, far above the detector
  trained on the simulator's sparse API labels, whose low recall ($0.64$) would
  bottleneck the pipeline.}
  \label{fig:yolo}

\subsection{The Effect of Foreground Extraction}
\label{sec:results-norm}

Figure~\ref{fig:norm} isolates the normalization problem of
Section~\ref{sec:frustum}. The baseline maximum-distance normalization yields a
$3$D RMSE of $2.94$~m, a detection precision of only $0.21$, and a near-zero
AMOTA of $0.04$: the background-dominated frustum cripples both localization and
classification. Replacing the scale by a robust $95$th-percentile estimate barely
helps (RMSE $2.87$~m, AMOTA $0.05$), confirming that clipping the scale is the
wrong fix: the centroid is still pulled by the background. Removing the
background with the depth band reduces the error to $1.20$~m and lifts precision
to $0.90$ and AMOTA to $0.81$; the learned T-Net segmentation does better still,
$1.05$~m, while recovering the foreground without a manual cut. The dominant lever
in the frustum pipeline is thus foreground extraction, not the regressor.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap7/normalization_ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Effect of foreground extraction on the frustum detector. Robust
  normalization (p95) does not help; removing the background (depth band) or
  learning it (T-Net segmentation) reduces the 3D error from $3.0$~m to
  ${\approx}1.0$~m and raises precision and AMOTA accordingly.}
  \label{fig:norm}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Detection and Tracking}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:mainresults}~(left) report the full
pipeline (YOLO $\rightarrow$ frustum $\rightarrow$ PointNet with foreground
extraction) on the campaign. The system localizes targets to a global RMSE of $1.20$~m
and tracks them with an AMOTA of $0.75$--$0.88$ across the three
environments, with very high precision ($0.83$--$0.99$). Two patterns emerge.
First, AMOTA is high while the single-point MOTA at $0.5$ is modest
($0.25$--$0.56$): the gap is the signature of a strong tracker fed by a
recall-limited detector. AMOTA, integrating over recall, rewards the chain when
the target is seen, whereas MOTA at a single threshold is dragged down by the missed
detections. Second, the localization error is essentially flat with range
(Figure~\ref{fig:mainresults}, right: $1.25$~m at $0$--$30$~m, $1.16$~m at $30$--$50$~m,
$1.30$~m at $50$--$90$~m), which is the property a sense-and-avoid system needs
and which mirrors the range-bounded behavior of the tracker's Cram\'er--Rao
analysis in Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}. The recall ($0.31$--$0.58$, lowest in the
tree-cluttered \textsc{AirSimNH} and under fog) is inherited almost entirely from
the 2D stage and is the principal limitation of the two-stage design.

\subsection{Architectural Comparison: Frustum, Voxel, and Fusion}
\label{sec:results-arch}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap7/approaches_diagram.pdf}
  \caption{The three detection paradigms compared in this chapter.
  (a)~Two-stage frustum: the YOLO detector gates a frustum that a
  PointNet\texttt{++} refines into a 3D box, precise but recall-limited by the 2D
  stage. (b)~Single-stage voxel: the whole point cloud is pillarized and a 2D
  convolutional backbone with a center-based head detects directly in 3D, higher
  recall but noisier. (c)~Late fusion: voxel detections complement the precise
  frustum tracks.}
  \label{fig:approaches}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:arch} and Figure~\ref{fig:arch} compare the frustum variants
against the single-stage voxel detectors and a late fusion of the two. The three
paradigms are contrasted side by side in Figure~\ref{fig:approaches}: the
two-stage frustum pipeline, the single-stage voxel detector, and their late
fusion. The two
paradigms occupy opposite regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}). The frustum methods
are precise but recall-limited: the T-Net segmentation and the depth band attain
the highest frustum AMOTA ($0.81$) and precision ($0.90$), but their recall is
capped near $0.49$ by the 2D detector. The voxel methods, detecting in the whole
scene without a 2D proposal, reach a far higher recall ($0.76$--$0.80$) and
detection $F_1$ ($0.79$--$0.83$); among them, replacing the hand-crafted pillar
features by a learned encoder (PFN) is the decisive change, cutting the
localization error from $1.19$~m to $0.87$~m, the best of \emph{any} method,
frustum or voxel. A dense 3D backbone matches it ($0.92$~m) and confirms that the
gain comes from the learned local representation rather than grid resolution. The
two negative controls bear this out: a finer grid does not help (the bottleneck is
point sparsity on the target, not quantization) and naive temporal accumulation
hurts (ego-only compensation smears the moving target across cells). The voxel
detectors remain noisier in identity (higher switch counts), which is why their
AMOTA ($0.63$--$0.68$) trails the frustum's despite their stronger single-frame
detection. A late fusion that confirms frustum detections with voxel ones lifts
recall to $0.66$ at the frustum's precision, giving the best AMOTA among
high-recall configurations ($0.75$).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments establish three points. First, \emph{the synthetic dataset is
viable}: pixel-exact composition avoids the simulator's annotation defects and
trains a highly accurate 2D detector, and the geometric 3D annotation supplies
trustworthy supervision for the point-cloud stage. Second, \emph{foreground
extraction, not the regressor, governs frustum localization}: removing or learning
the background takes the 3D error from $3$~m to about $1$~m, and a learned
segmentation is the principled successor to the depth-band heuristic. Third,
\emph{the choice of detection paradigm is a recall--precision trade-off}: the
two-stage frustum design is precise and tracks well but inherits the 2D detector's
recall ceiling, whereas the single-stage voxel design, particularly with
learned pillar features, recalls more targets and localizes them best, at the
cost of noisier identities; a late fusion captures much of both.

For the detect-and-avoid application this trade-off has a clear resolution: a
missed obstacle is far more dangerous than a noisier estimate of a detected one,
so \emph{recall should be favored over localization precision}. This argues for
the single-stage voxel detector (or the late fusion), which recall many more
targets at a modest cost in localization noise, rather than the frustum pipeline
alone, despite the latter's higher AMOTA at low recall.

These results connect directly to the rest of the pipeline. The 3D detections
produced here are exactly the spherical measurements
$[\rho,\alpha,\beta,r]$ that the EKF of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} ingests, and
the range-flat localization error mirrors the tracker's range-dependent
uncertainty model, so the detector and the filter are matched by design. The
principal limitation is the recall of the two-stage front-end under clutter and
fog, which the single-stage and fusion variants are shown to relieve. The
remaining gap to deployment is the sim-to-real transfer: the detectors are trained
purely in simulation, and validating them on real aerial footage, as the
monocular tracker of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular} is validated on real
flights, is the natural next step. A second gap is computational: the 2D stage
runs in about $5$~ms per frame on a desktop GPU, but the end-to-end latency of the
full pipeline was not benchmarked, and the farthest-point sampling in the
PointNet\texttt{++} stage scales quadratically with the $N=4096$ frustum points,
so an onboard-compute optimization (and a measured real-time budget) is required
before an embedded sense-and-avoid deployment.
