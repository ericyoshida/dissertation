The three studies that form the body of this dissertation address different
problems: a simulated range--bearing tracker (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}), a
real-world monocular tracker (Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}), and a synthetic
dataset with a 3D detector (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}). Yet they are not three
separate works. They are three contributions to a single artefact: the perception
(\emph{Sense}) pipeline that turns onboard sensing into calibrated
three-dimensional tracks for detect-and-avoid. This chapter presents that pipeline
once, as a whole, and shows where each subsequent chapter plugs in. It is the hub
that makes the rest of the dissertation read as one system rather than three
appendices.

\section{Overview: A Modular Camera--Point-Cloud Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline-overview}

The pipeline realizes the \emph{Sense} capability of
Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork} as a cascade of four stages, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. A monocular image is first reduced to a set of
two-dimensional object detections; each detection is lifted into a viewing
frustum that bounds the corresponding volume of the point cloud; a point-cloud
network estimates an amodal three-dimensional box within that frustum; and a
recursive Bayesian filter tracks the resulting detections over time, emitting a
state estimate together with its covariance. The design follows the
camera--point-cloud fusion lineage of Frustum~PointNets \cite{qi2018frustum},
adapted to the aerial sense-and-avoid setting and terminated by the tracking stage
that the literature most often defers (Chapter~\ref{cha:relatedwork}).

Two design commitments run through the whole pipeline. The first is
\emph{fusion by geometry}: rather than fusing camera and point-cloud features in a
learned latent space, the image detection geometrically constrains the
point-cloud search through the camera model of Chapter~\ref{cha:background},
keeping each modality interpretable and each stage independently trainable. The
second is \emph{calibrated output}: the pipeline does not merely report where an
obstacle is, but how uncertain that estimate is, because the downstream avoidance
logic must reason about collision risk. This is why the safety-critical final
stage is a Bayesian filter and not a regressor, and why its consistency, not
just its accuracy, is analysed in Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking}
and~\ref{cha:monocular}.

\section{The Four Stages at a Glance}
\label{sec:pipeline-stages}

Each stage is summarized conceptually here, with its inputs, method, and
outputs; the detailed methodology is developed in the project chapters, so as not
to duplicate it.

\subsection{Two-Dimensional Detection}
The first stage maps the RGB image to a set of object bounding boxes with
confidence scores. It uses a one-stage convolutional detector of the YOLO family
\cite{redmon2016yolo}, chosen for the real-time throughput an onboard system
demands. Operating in the image alone, it provides high-recall \emph{where}
information at low cost but no metric depth; its boxes are proposals that the next
stage lifts into three dimensions. Its training and accuracy are treated in
Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}.

\subsection{Frustum Proposal}
Each 2D box, together with the camera intrinsics of
Chapter~\ref{cha:background}, defines a viewing frustum, the cone of space that
projects into the box. Intersecting this frustum with the point cloud yields a
small, target-centred subset of points, reducing an unordered scene of $10^5$
points to a few thousand that plausibly belong to one object. This is the
geometric fusion step: the image localizes the search, the point cloud supplies
the depth. It carries no learned parameters and is detailed in
Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}.

\subsection{Three-Dimensional Detection}
A PointNet-style network \cite{qi2017pointnetpp} consumes the frustum points and
regresses an amodal 3D bounding box (centre, size, and an objectness score that
rejects frustums seeded by a false positive). Operating on the raw point set, it is
invariant to point ordering and recovers metric position where the monocular image
could not. Its output, expressed as a range, azimuth, elevation, and size, is the
measurement consumed by the tracker. The architecture, its failure modes, and
principled alternatives are the subject of Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}.

\subsection{Tracking}
The final stage is an Extended Kalman Filter
(Chapter~\ref{cha:background}) that fuses the stream of per-frame 3D detections
into a temporally coherent state (position, velocity, and size) with an
explicit covariance. It rejects spurious detections through gating, bridges
missed detections through prediction, and reports a calibrated
uncertainty. Because the sensor rides a moving platform, the filter must account
for the observer's own motion; this is the problem analysed in depth in
Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:monocular}.

\section{Modularity and Cross-Domain Generality}
\label{sec:pipeline-modularity}

The pipeline is deliberately modular: the interfaces between stages are explicit
and low-dimensional (boxes, then frustum points, then a 3D measurement with
covariance), so each stage can be trained, validated, and replaced
independently. This is what makes the three studies composable rather than
monolithic, and it carries two further benefits.

First, \emph{independent validation}: a stage can be analysed against its own
ground truth without an end-to-end system. The tracker can be proved correct and
benchmarked against the Cram\'er--Rao bound in simulation
(Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}) before any detector is attached; the detector can be
evaluated on detection and localization metrics (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim})
independently of the filter. The safety-critical stage thus receives the
mathematical scrutiny it warrants in isolation.

Second, \emph{cross-domain generality}: because the tracker consumes an abstract
range--bearing measurement with a covariance, it does not care whether that
measurement came from a camera--LiDAR fusion detecting a drone or from a monocular
detector localizing a person. The same estimator therefore serves both the 3D
obstacle-tracking task of Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} and the monocular
person-tracking task of Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}, a designed feature of the
modular interface rather than a coincidence. The two domains differ in whether
the platform pose is known, which is exactly the condition that
Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} identifies as the boundary between the equivalent and
the divergent regimes of the filter.

\section{Mapping the Contributions onto the Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline-mapping}

The three self-contained studies of this dissertation each contribute one part of
the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}; together they constitute the
\emph{one vision, three contributions} structure of the work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The tracking stage} (Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking}). The
  safety-critical filter is formulated for a moving platform, its global- and
  local-frame variants are proved equivalent under known pose and isotropic
  process noise, the regimes of genuine divergence are characterized, and the
  filter is benchmarked against the Cram\'er--Rao bound in simulation. This is the
  most mature contribution and is developed first.
  \item \textbf{The tracking stage under uncertain pose}
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:monocular}). The same estimator is taken to real flight data,
  where the platform pose is \emph{estimated} rather than known; the filter
  propagates the camera-pose uncertainty into its innovation, instantiating the
  divergent regime that Chapter~\ref{cha:tracking} predicts and demonstrating the
  generality of the framework on a different sensor (monocular) and target
  (person).
  \item \textbf{The detection front-end and its data}
  (Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}). The 2D detector, frustum proposal, and 3D detector are
  designed and trained, and the synthetic dataset that makes their training
  possible is built; the chapter also compares the adopted two-stage frustum
  design against the single-stage voxel alternative on a moving-observer campaign.
\end{itemize}

In short, Chapters~\ref{cha:tracking} and~\ref{cha:monocular} build and validate
the tracking stage in two complementary regimes, and Chapter~\ref{cha:airsim}
builds the detection stages that feed it. The chapters that follow can therefore
be read independently, each as a complete study, while this pipeline keeps them
anchored to a single end-to-end system.
